The Competitive Firm: How a Price-Taker Decides

Why a small seller takes the market price, how it picks its output, when it shuts down or leaves, and how the firms' choices add up to market supply.

Class meeting 17 · October 22, 2026 · 10-minute review

A competitive firm is so small next to its market that it cannot change the price. It takes the market price as given and decides only how much to make. Where its marginal cost is rising, it makes each unit that adds more to revenue than to cost, so its rising marginal cost is its supply curve. Average cost answers a different question: whether to make anything at all today, and whether to stay in business when the lease or the equipment comes up for renewal. Add up what every firm makes at each price and you get the market supply curve.

The four-step diagnostic

Use these steps whenever a question gives you a price-taking firm, its costs, and a market price.

  1. Read the price as marginal revenue.
    A price-taker sells every unit at the market price, so each extra unit adds exactly the price to revenue. At $25, revenue is $175 for 7 units and $200 for 8: marginal revenue is $25, the price.
  2. Find how much to make.
    Look where marginal cost rises through the price, not where it is still falling. Make every unit up to the last one on the rising part that costs less than the price, and stop before the first one that costs more. The cheap units come with the costlier first units that lead to them.
  3. Ask whether to make anything at all.
    Today, compare the price with the lowest average variable cost (the shutdown price). When the lease or the equipment must be renewed, compare it with the lowest average total cost (the break-even price).
  4. Add up the firms.
    Market supply at a price is the sum of what every firm makes at that price. A firm whose shutdown price is above the market price makes nothing.

What you should be able to do

  1. Explain why a price-taking firm sells nothing above the market price and has no reason to charge less.
  2. Compute total revenue and marginal revenue, and explain why marginal revenue equals the price for a price-taker.
  3. Use marginal cost and the price to find how many units a firm makes, and read its supply curve off its marginal cost.
  4. Tell fixed cost from variable cost, and average variable cost from average total cost.
  5. Decide whether a firm runs or shuts down today, and whether it stays or leaves in the long run.
  6. Compute what a firm loses if it runs and if it shuts down.
  7. Add individual firms' quantities to get market supply, and explain why short-run and long-run supply differ.

A small seller takes the market price

What a price-taker is

Economists call a firm like this a competitive firm. The instructor does not love the name, since every firm that uses scarce resources faces competition, even a monopoly. What the name means is that the firm is a price-taker: its own output is too small to move the market price. Any firm can post any price it wants; the question is whether it sells anything. Publix could charge $100 for a gallon of milk, but people would buy their milk somewhere else. If Walmart in Boca sells more milk or less, the price of milk in Boca does not change. The opposite case, a price-maker or price-searcher (often called a monopoly), can move the price of what it sells. That comes after the exam.

Suppose the firm is one of hundreds selling the same product, and the market price is $25. The firm does not face the market’s downward-sloping demand curve. The demand curve it faces is a flat line at $25. If it asks $25.01, buyers go to a rival next door, just as no one buys gas at the station that charges more than the identical station beside it. It sells nothing. Would it ever charge less, say $24.50? No: it can sell all it wants at $25, so every sale at $24.50 throws away 50 cents.

Marginal revenue is the price

Total revenue is price times quantity. Seven units at $25 bring in $175; eight bring in $200. Marginal revenue is the change in total revenue from one more unit: $200 − $175 = $25, the price. A price-taker can sell more without pushing the price down, and it cannot push the price up by holding output back. So for a price-taker, price and marginal revenue are the same thing, for the ninth unit, the tenth, and every unit after.

How many units at $25? · TopHat

Unit 7 pays its way; unit 8 does not

The firm’s marginal costs for units 1 to 9 are $9, $8, $6, $5, $7, $13, $22, $30, and $40. The first unit brings in $25 and costs $9, so it adds $16. The second costs $8 and adds even more. Keep going unit by unit. The seventh costs $22, below $25, so it adds $3. The eighth costs $30: making it would leave the firm $5 worse off, and the ninth $15 worse off. So the firm stops at seven. The instructor put the rule as “marginal revenue equals marginal cost, if you can.” With whole units the two are rarely exactly equal, so in practice: where marginal cost is rising, make every unit that costs less than the price, and stop before the first one that costs more.

TopHat check. How many units should the firm make at $25? The answer is 7. “4, because the fourth unit costs the least” is wrong: the fifth, sixth, and seventh units also cost less than $25, so each adds to profit. “5, because costs start to rise after that” is wrong for the same reason: the sixth unit costs $13 and sells for $25, so it leaves the firm $12 better off. “8” is wrong because the eighth unit loses $5. (The first TopHat question, on the $25.01 price, was dropped.)

The firm’s supply curve is its marginal cost

Given the price and the firm’s marginal costs, you can tell how much it makes. At $15 it makes 6 units (the seventh costs $22). At $25 it makes 7. At $35 it makes 8 (the ninth costs $40). As the price rises, the firm is willing to make more. Its rising marginal cost is its supply curve, and anything that moves the market price moves the amount the firm makes.

The price falls to $8 · TopHat

Fixed and variable costs; two averages

Suppose you open a restaurant in a leased building. You owe the lease even if you never sell a sandwich: that is a fixed cost. The workers and the bread, meat, and cheese go up with the number of sandwiches: those are variable costs. Average variable cost (AVC) is variable cost divided by output: the running cost per unit. Average total cost (ATC) is total cost, fixed plus variable, divided by output. ATC sits above AVC because it includes the fixed cost. In the slide’s firm, the equipment costs $10 a day and cannot be resold.

Both averages are U-shaped. At first, making more each day spreads the setup costs, so cost per unit falls. Economists call this economies of scale. Later, cost per unit rises. Marginal cost pulls the averages the way a newcomer changes the average height in a classroom: a shorter student pulls the average down, a taller one pulls it up. So marginal cost crosses AVC and ATC at their lowest points. In the slide’s firm, the lowest AVC is $7 and the lowest ATC is $9. AVC is $7 at both 4 and 5 units; ATC is lowest at 5.

At $8 the firm makes 5 units

Now the price falls to $8 and is expected to stay there. Marginal revenue is $8. The fifth unit costs $7 and the sixth $13, so the firm makes 5 units. The instructor’s trick question: the price line also meets marginal cost back at the first units, where marginal cost is still falling. That is not the answer. Look for the place where marginal cost rises through the price.

TopHat check. At $8, with the lowest AVC at $7, the lowest ATC at $9, and $10 a day of equipment that cannot be resold, what does the firm do? Most of the class got it right: it keeps producing today, but will not replace the equipment when it wears out.

ItemAmount
Revenue (5 × $8)$40

If it runs: 40 − 35 − 10−$5
If it shuts down: 0 − 10−$10

Running loses $5. Shutting down saves the $35 running cost but not the $10, which is already spent, so it loses $10. In the short run the equipment cost is a sunk cost, the idea from the start of the semester: it is owed either way, so it does not affect today’s choice. That is why a firm can sensibly run at a loss for a while. When the lease comes up for renewal or the machine wears out, the $10 becomes a choice again, and at $8 it does not pay.

The shutdown price and the break-even price

Below $7 (the lowest AVC), the firm cannot cover even its running cost, so it shuts down at once. That is the shutdown price. Between $7 and $9, it runs what it has, but when the lease or the equipment is up for renewal, it does not renew: it leaves. At $9 or more (the lowest ATC, the break-even price), it covers all its costs and stays. In the long run every cost is a variable cost. Average cost does not tell the firm how much to make, but it tells it whether to be in the market at all.

A student asked a follow-up about the long run. The instructor’s answer: if you expect the $8 price and your costs to last for years, leave. You might first look for a cheaper lease; if rents fall, your fixed cost falls, the whole ATC curve shifts down, and you might stay. Losses are a signal from the market: the resources you use would be worth more somewhere else. Profits signal the opposite.

From one firm to market supply · TopHat

Three firms at $20

Market supply is built the same way as market demand: add up the individuals. The market supply of gas in Boca is the sum of what every gas station will sell at each price. A firm’s own supply curve is its marginal cost above its shutdown price: the lowest AVC in the short run, and the lowest ATC in the long run.

On the slide, three firms stand in for the many firms in a market. Their shutdown prices are $6 for A, $18 for B, and $28 for C. At a price of $20, A and B produce and C does not. The slide’s curves give A 18 units and B 12, so the market supplies 30. (In class, the instructor read rougher numbers off the chart; use the slide’s 18 and 12.)

TopHat check. At $20, how many units does the market supply? The answer is 30: 18 from A plus 12 from B. C makes nothing, because $20 is below its $28 shutdown price.

Adding the firms; the short run and the long run

Below $6, nobody produces. From $6, firm A alone is the market. At $18, B joins, and market supply is A plus B. At $28, C joins. At $30, A makes 22, B 17, and C 5: 44 in all. The lowest-cost firm enters first; the highest-cost firm, C, needs a high price before it will produce. A student added that the more firms join, the flatter the market curve gets, and the instructor agreed.

This is short-run supply: the set of firms is fixed, and the question for each is whether to produce. In the long run, firms can leave, and new firms can enter. The instructor’s example: if two big AI companies were earning large profits, you would expect new rivals to enter. During COVID, many restaurants stopped selling for a while because the price did not cover their running cost, but they did not leave the market. They expected demand to return, and when it did, they reopened. Shutting down is a short-run choice; exiting is a long-run one.

Check your reasoning

Answer before you open each one. Every question uses only material from class, with new numbers.

Question 1 — A small egg farm is one of thousands. Eggs sell for $3 a dozen. What happens if the farm asks $3.10? Why would it not ask $2.90? What is its revenue from 40 dozen, from 41 dozen, and its marginal revenue from the 41st dozen?

At $3.10 it sells nothing: buyers get the same eggs from other farms at $3. At $2.90 it would give up 10 cents on every dozen it could have sold at $3. Revenue is 40 × $3 = $120 and 41 × $3 = $123, so marginal revenue is $3, the price.

Question 2 — A price-taking firm sells at $40. Its marginal costs for units 1 to 8 are $31, $24, $19, $21, $27, $35, $45, and $56. How many units should it make, and how much do those units add above their cost? A friend says to stop at 3, because the third unit is the cheapest. Is the friend right?

Make 6 units. The sixth costs $35, below $40; the seventh costs $45, above it. The six units add $9 + $16 + $21 + $19 + $13 + $5 = $83 (revenue $240 minus running cost $157). The friend is wrong: units 4, 5, and 6 cost more than unit 3, but each still costs less than $40, so each adds to profit.

Question 3 — Same firm as Question 2. How many units does it make at $32? At $50? What do these answers show?

At $32: 5 (the fifth costs $27, the sixth $35). At $50: 7 (the seventh costs $45, the eighth $56). As the price rises, the firm makes more: its rising marginal cost is its supply curve.

Question 4 — Same firm. Its average variable cost is $31.00 for 1 unit, $27.50 for 2, $24.67 for 3, $23.75 for 4, and $24.40 for 5. What is its shutdown price? At a price of $26, the first unit costs $31, more than the price. How many units does the firm make, and does it cover its running cost?

The shutdown price is the lowest AVC, $23.75, at 4 units. (The fourth unit costs $21, below the average, so it pulls the average down; the fifth costs $27, above it, so it pulls the average up.) At $26 the firm makes 4 units: the fourth costs $21 and the fifth $27. Do not stop at the first unit: look where marginal cost rises through the price. Revenue is 4 × $26 = $104 against running cost of $95, so it covers its running cost with $9 to spare.

Question 5 — A car wash charges the market price, $11 a wash, and does 200 washes a day. At 200 washes, its average variable cost is $8.50 and its average total cost is $12. Its lease is signed for the year. How much does it lose if it runs? If it closes for now? What should it do?

Revenue is $2,200 and running cost $1,700, so $500 is left over toward the fixed cost of ($12 − $8.50) × 200 = $700. Running loses $200. Closing saves the $1,700 but not the $700 lease, so it loses $700. It should keep running for now, since the price covers its average variable cost.

Question 6 — A bakery’s lowest average variable cost is $12 a loaf and its lowest average total cost is $15. For each price, what does it do today, and what does it do when its lease comes up for renewal: $11, $13, and $16?

$11: below the $12 shutdown price, so it shuts down now. $13: it covers its running cost, so it keeps baking today, but it is below the $15 break-even price, so it does not renew the lease and leaves. $16: it covers all its costs, so it renews and stays.

Question 7 — Three price-taking firms make the same product. Their shutdown prices are $4 for X, $13 for Y, and $16 for Z. At $14, X makes 40 units and Y makes 25. At $18, X makes 45, Y makes 30, and Z makes 10. What is market supply at $14 and at $18?

At $14: 40 + 25 = 65. Z makes nothing, because $14 is below its $16 shutdown price. At $18: 45 + 30 + 10 = 85. A higher price draws more from each firm and switches on the highest-cost firm.

Question 8 — In a winter with no snow, the price a ski-rental shop can charge falls below its lowest average variable cost. The owner expects snow next winter. Should the shop shut down, exit the market, or both? What would change your answer?

Shut down, but do not exit. Below the lowest AVC, every rental loses money on running cost alone, so it stops renting for now. But the lease and the gear are already paid for, and the owner expects the price to recover, like the restaurants that closed during COVID and later reopened. If the owner expected the price to stay below the lowest average total cost for good, the shop would leave when its lease came up for renewal.

Question 9 — A firm’s average variable cost is $15 at 10 units. The 11th unit costs $12 and the 12th costs $20. What happens to average variable cost at 11 and 12 units? What does this tell you about where the lowest AVC is?

Variable cost at 10 units is $150. At 11 units: ($150 + $12) ÷ 11 = $14.73, lower, because the new unit costs less than the average. At 12 units: ($162 + $20) ÷ 12 = $15.17, higher, because the new unit costs more. The lowest AVC is near 11 units, where marginal cost moves from below the average to above it.

Bottom line

A price-taker cannot choose its price, only its output. Where its marginal cost is rising, it makes each unit that costs less than the price, so its rising marginal cost is its supply curve. It keeps running today if the price covers its lowest average variable cost, and it stays in the long run only if the price covers its lowest average total cost. Add up the firms at each price and you have the market supply curve.
