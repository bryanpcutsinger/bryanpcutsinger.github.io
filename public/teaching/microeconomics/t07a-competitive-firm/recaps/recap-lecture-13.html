Entry, Exit, and Where Profit Goes

How profit draws new firms in, how losses push firms out, why the long-run price ends at the lowest average total cost, and where the profit goes when the city caps entry.

Class meeting 20 · November 3, 2026 · 10-minute review

On this page

Profit is a signal. When demand rises, the firms already in a market earn a profit. If anyone can set up the same kind of firm, new firms come in, their output pushes the price down, and entry stops when the price is back at the lowest average total cost and profit is zero. Losses work the other way, more slowly, as equipment wears out. If the law blocks entry, the profit does not vanish: it becomes the price of whatever gives the right to enter, such as a taxi medallion.

The four-step diagnostic

Use these steps whenever a question gives you a market of price-taking firms and a change in demand or in the rules for entry.

  1. Find each firm’s profit at the current price.
    Profit = quantity × (price − average total cost). The cost includes an ordinary return on the owner’s money and time, so zero economic profit means the owner does as well here as anywhere else.
  2. Read the signal.
    A profit draws new firms in, if they can enter. A price between the lowest average variable cost and the lowest average total cost means a loss: firms keep running but leave as their equipment wears out. A price below the lowest average variable cost means they shut down at once.
  3. Find where entry or exit stops.
    When every firm, new or old, has the same costs and entry does not raise input prices, entry or exit stops when the price is back at the lowest average total cost. The number of firms = the quantity buyers want at that price ÷ what each firm makes there.
  4. If entry is blocked, follow the profit.
    Competition moves to whatever gives the right to enter. Would-be entrants bid its rent up to the full profit, so whoever uses it earns an ordinary return, and the gain goes to those who held it when the profit appeared.

What you should be able to do

  1. Explain why average revenue equals the price for a price-taking firm, and compute profit as quantity times (price minus average total cost).
  2. Tell economic profit from accounting profit, and explain what zero economic profit means.
  3. Explain how a rise in demand first raises the price and each firm’s output, and how entry then brings the price back down.
  4. Find the long-run price and the number of firms after entry or exit stops.
  5. Explain why a firm losing money keeps running for a while, and why exit is slower than entry.
  6. Draw short-run and long-run supply together and explain why long-run supply is flat in a constant-cost industry.
  7. Explain how a cap on entry turns a profit into a rent, who gains, who loses, and why reform is hard.

Where we left off

The price-taking firm

A price-taking firm accepts the market price as given. It sells every unit at that price, so its marginal revenue equals the price, and it makes units up to where price equals marginal cost. Even then it can lose money in the short run: it may cover its variable costs but not its fixed costs. If it cannot cover even its variable costs, it shuts down at once. Today’s question: how do firms come into and leave a market?

One hundred firms, then a rise in demand · TopHat

One hundred firms just break even at $9

Start with 100 identical firms, each with Lecture 12’s costs. The market price is $9, so each makes 5 units and the market makes 500. Is anyone earning a profit? On the board, the instructor showed that average revenue is total revenue divided by quantity: (price × quantity) ÷ quantity = the price. So profit = quantity × (price − average total cost). At $9 the price equals the lowest average total cost, so profit is zero. (In class the instructor wrote price, not quantity, in front of the parentheses. The multiplier is the quantity.)

Zero economic profit does not mean zero accounting profit. It means the owners do as well as in their next-best choice. The instructor’s example: suppose the stock market (the S&P 500) pays about 7 percent a year after inflation. A business that earns 7 percent earns zero economic profit, because the same money could earn 7 percent in the stock market. A business that earns 5 percent should close in the long run and move the money. Quarterly earnings reports, such as Apple’s, show accounting profit, not economic profit.

Demand rises and the price jumps to $25

Now demand rises. New firms take time to set up: they need space, workers, and the paperwork for a new business. So for now the market still has 100 firms, and supply is their marginal costs added up. The price rises to $25, and each firm moves up its marginal cost curve to 7 units. Average total cost at 7 units is $11.43, so profit is 7 × ($25 − $11.43) = $95 a day (sales $175, running cost $70, equipment $10). The average total cost curve does not shift: the lease and the contracts are already signed.

No one is in charge of this. Buyers want more, so the price rises, and the higher price and the profit that comes with it tell the firms to make more.

TopHat check. If anyone can buy the same equipment and match these costs, and entry does not raise input prices, where does the price end up? Most of the class got it right: $9, the lowest average total cost. It cannot settle at $7: at any price below $9 firms lose money, some leave, supply falls, and the price rises.

Entry · TopHat

A $95 profit draws new firms in

People outside the market see $95 a day and set up the same equipment. If a business here earns 15 percent when the stock market pays 7, money moves out of the stock market and into this business. On the slide, 50 firms have entered: supply shifts right, the price falls to $17, and each firm makes 6 units and $44 a day. That is still a profit, so entry goes on. As the number of firms rises, total output rises, even though each firm makes less.

TopHat check. At $9 buyers want 1,100 units a day, and a firm that just breaks even makes 5. How many firms are there when entry stops? 1,100 ÷ 5 = 220, up from 100.

In the long run, supply is flat at $9

Entry stops when the price is back at $9 and profit is zero again. The market now makes 1,100 units at the old price. The instructor called this a constant-cost industry: every new firm has the same costs, and entry does not raise what firms pay for inputs. Then long-run supply is flat (perfectly elastic) at the lowest average total cost. A price above $9 lasts only until new firms arrive. The firms end up with an ordinary return, so the gain from the larger market goes to buyers.

Exit · TopHat

Suppose instead the price falls to $8

Start again from 100 firms at $9, and suppose demand falls for good. The price drops to $8. Each firm still makes 5 units, because $8 beats its $7 running cost per unit. It loses $5 a day, against $10 if it shut down (Lecture 12). So in the short run the firms stay. But $8 is below the $9 lowest average total cost, so replacing worn-out equipment does not pay. Firms leave as their equipment wears out, supply shrinks, and the price climbs back to $9. On the slide, 5 firms leave: at $9 buyers want 475 units, from 95 firms.

TopHat check. What happens over the next few years? Firms keep running, leave as their equipment wears out, and the price climbs back to $9. “Some leave and the price stays at $8” is half right: as firms leave, supply falls, so the price must rise. “All 100 shut down at once” is wrong: at $8 they cover their running cost, so shutting down would lose more. “Nothing changes” is wrong: the firms do not replace their equipment.

Losses are a signal too. A loss means the price buyers pay does not cover the cost of the resources, so those resources would be worth more somewhere else.

Short-run and long-run supply together

Points A, B, and C

One chart holds the whole story. At A, 100 firms sell 500 units at $9. Demand rises. New firms cannot start at once, so the firms already there take up the extra demand by moving up their marginal costs: the price jumps along short-run supply to B ($25, 700 units). The profit draws firms in, short-run supply moves right, and the price falls until C ($9, 1,100 units, 220 firms). Long-run supply joins A and C. A fall in demand runs the other way: the price falls first, then exit brings it back up. In the short run the price does the adjusting; in the long run the number of firms does. In a constant-cost industry, the cost conditions, not demand, set the long-run price.

Taxi medallions · TopHat × 2

Suppose the city issues only 100 medallions

Go back to the rise in demand, but now the firms are taxis: a unit is a ride, the price is the fare, and the $10 a day of equipment is the cab. To pick up riders, a cab needs a medallion, a license from the city, and the city has issued only 100. Without the cap, new cabs would enter until the fare fell back to $9. With it, the 101st cab can never enter, so the fare stays at $25 and each holder earns $95 a day. A student had pointed out earlier that firms earning a profit would want to block entry; the medallion is one way to do it.

Would-be drivers bid for the medallions

Many people want the $95 a day, but the law keeps them out unless they get a medallion. So they compete for the medallions instead, and bid until a medallion rents for the full $95 a day. (A buyer who purchases one outright pays today for that whole stream of $95 a day.) The instructor’s comparison: when he auctioned a $20 bill in small classes, the price went to $20.

TopHat check. A new driver rents a medallion for $95 a day. Sales are $175, running cost $70, and the cab $10. What is the driver’s profit? Most of the class got it right: $0. The driver pays $95 to get the $95.

The profit became the medallion’s rent

The holder earns zero profit too, once you count the opportunity cost: driving with your own medallion means giving up the $95 a day someone else would pay for it. Counting the rent, average total cost is ($70 + $10 + $95) ÷ 7 = $25, equal to the fare. The gain went to the people who held medallions when the profit appeared, as a one-time rise in the medallion’s value (a capital gain). Its price equals the present value of $95 a day into the future. Economists say the profit is capitalized into the price of the asset.

Competition still takes profit to zero. With free entry, it does so by adding cabs and pushing the fare down. With a cap, it does so by bidding up the medallion’s rent. The difference is for riders: with free entry the fare falls back to $9; with the cap it stays at $25.

Questions from students

A student asked why the fare does not fall back. Because the city allows only 100 medallions, the number of cabs cannot grow. On the A-B-C chart, the market goes from A to B and stops there: the existing drivers give more rides, but no new cabs join. Another student asked whether the city could forbid selling the medallions. The instructor’s answer: then they would probably be sold under the table, and in practice the city did let them be bought and sold. A third student asked him to go over who does what. The city creates the cap; if the license can be sold, its value reflects the profit the cap creates, and that value becomes a cost to anyone who keeps using it.

The instructor’s real-world note (not on the slides): before Uber, a New York taxi medallion was a driver’s retirement plan. A new driver took out a loan to buy one, drove for years, and sold it on retirement to the next driver. By his rough recollection, medallions sold for something like $700,000 to $1 million.

TopHat check. The city decides to issue many more medallions. Who loses? The people who hold medallions now. More cabs mean a lower fare, so the rent a medallion earns falls, and so does its value. Riders gain from the lower fare. People who want to start driving do not lose: they no longer pay $95 a day for the right to drive.

Who fights more medallions?

The cap, not the rent, keeps the fare at $25; a ride still costs as little as $9 to provide. Lift the cap, and new cabs enter until the fare is $9 and a medallion is worth nothing. Riders gain. Current holders lose, so they oppose more medallions, even though more medallions would be better for riders. This makes reform hard. The people who gained when the cap began may be long gone. Today’s holders paid a price that already counted the profit, so they earn only an ordinary return, yet they lose if the cap ends. Economists call this the transitional gains trap: once a privilege can be bought and sold, it is very hard to take away.

The last slide

The recording stopped as this slide came up, so here is what the slide says. (1) Each firm makes units out to where its rising marginal cost passes the price, so the last unit made is worth at least as much to its buyer as it cost to make. (2) With free entry, and entrants like these, the price settles at the lowest average total cost, $9: the product is made at the lowest cost per unit. (3) No manager needs to know marginal cost to the penny. Firms that keep losing money tend to change or leave; profitable methods tend to grow and get copied. Luck and finance matter too.

This holds only when prices include every cost and no one outside the trade bears one (Lecture 8). A taxi medallion cap breaks point 2: it keeps out rides that riders value above their $9 cost.

Check your reasoning

These use new numbers. Try each one before you open the answer.

Question 1 — A price-taking firm sells 30 units at $14 each. Its average total cost at 30 units is $10. What is its profit?

Profit = quantity × (price − average total cost) = 30 × ($14 − $10) = $120. If you multiplied by the price instead ($14 × $4 = $56), you used the wrong multiplier: the gap of $4 is earned on each of the 30 units.

Question 2 — An owner puts $200,000 into a bakery. After paying every other cost, including a wage for the owner’s own time, the bakery earns $10,000 a year. The best other use of the money pays 5 percent a year. What is the economic profit? What if the bakery earned $6,000?

The next-best choice pays 5% of $200,000 = $10,000 a year, so the bakery earns zero economic profit: an ordinary return, even though its accounting profit is $10,000. At $6,000 it has an economic loss of $4,000 a year, and an owner who cares only about the return would move the money in the long run.

Question 3 — In a market of identical firms, each firm’s lowest average total cost is $12, at 40 units a day. New firms can set up with the same costs, and entry does not raise input prices. The market starts with 200 firms. Then demand rises, and at $12 buyers now want 12,000 units a day. How many firms are there when entry stops, and how many are new?

Entry stops when the price is back at $12, where each firm makes 40 units. 12,000 ÷ 40 = 300 firms, so 100 are new. (Before the rise, 200 firms × 40 = 8,000 units.)

Question 4 — Same market. Right after the rise in demand, before any new firm arrives, what happens to the price and to each firm’s output? Where does the price end up?

In the short run the price rises above $12, and each of the 200 firms moves up its marginal cost and makes more than 40 units, so each earns a profit. That profit draws firms in, supply shifts right, and the price falls back to $12. In the long run each firm again makes 40 units; what changes is the number of firms.

Question 5 — Same firms: lowest average variable cost $10, lowest average total cost $12. Demand falls for good and the price drops to $11. What does each firm do today, and over the next few years? What if the price had dropped to $9.50?

At $11 each firm covers its running cost, so it keeps running today. But $11 is below the $12 lowest average total cost, so it does not replace its equipment when it wears out. Firms leave one by one, supply shrinks, and the price rises back to $12. At $9.50, below the $10 lowest average variable cost, the firms cannot cover even their running cost, so they shut down at once.

Question 6 — At $11 one of these firms makes 40 units. At 40 units its average variable cost is $10.50, and its equipment costs $60 a day, which is already paid. How much does it lose if it runs? If it shuts down?

Revenue is 40 × $11 = $440 and running cost 40 × $10.50 = $420, leaving $20 toward the $60. Running loses $40 a day. Shutting down saves the $420 but not the $60, so it loses $60. It keeps running until the equipment wears out. (Check: average total cost at 40 units is $10.50 + $60 ÷ 40 = $12.)

Question 7 — A city allows only 60 food-truck permits. Each truck sells 120 meals a day at $12: sales of $1,440, running cost of $840, and the truck $120 a day. Many people want to run a truck. What daily rent will a permit fetch? What profit does a new owner who rents one earn?

Without the rent, a truck earns $1,440 − $840 − $120 = $480 a day. Would-be owners bid for the permits until the rent is $480 a day. A new owner who pays it earns $0 profit: an ordinary return. The holder who runs a truck with an owned permit also earns zero economic profit, because the holder gives up the $480 a day of rent.

Question 8 — Same permits. Who actually gained from the cap? Why does a person who buys a permit outright later not gain?

The gain went to the people who held permits when the profit appeared: the value of their permits jumped. A later buyer pays today for the whole stream of $480 a day, so the profit is capitalized into the permit’s price, and the buyer earns only an ordinary return on what was paid.

Question 9 — Without the cap, a truck’s lowest average total cost would be $7.50 a meal. The city lifts the cap. Where does the meal price go in the long run, and what is a permit worth? Who gains, who loses, and who fights hardest?

New trucks enter until the price falls to $7.50, and a permit is worth nothing. Customers gain from the lower price. Current permit holders lose the value of their permits, so they fight hardest, even though most of them earn only an ordinary return. Each customer gains a little, so few customers organize. A would-be truck owner earns an ordinary return either way.

Question 10 — A constant-cost industry is at its long-run price. Demand falls for good. Describe what happens in the short run and in the long run. Which one changes for good: the price, the number of firms, or each firm’s output?

In the short run the price falls along short-run supply, and each firm makes a little less and loses money. Over time firms leave as their equipment wears out, and the price rises back to the lowest average total cost. In the long run each firm makes the same output at the same price as before; what changes for good is the number of firms.

Bottom line

Profit pulls firms in and losses push them out, until the price is back at the lowest average total cost and every firm earns an ordinary return. Entry comes as fast as new firms can set up; exit waits for equipment to wear out. If the law blocks entry, competition moves to the right to enter: its rent rises to the full profit, the people who held it when the profit appeared keep the gain, and buyers keep paying the higher price. No one has to plan any of it.
