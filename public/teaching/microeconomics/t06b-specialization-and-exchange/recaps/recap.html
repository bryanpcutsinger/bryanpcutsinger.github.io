Who Should Bake the Cake? Specialization, Gains from Trade, and Blocking Entry

Why someone who is worse at everything still has something to sell, how two traders split the gains, what a cheaper newcomer does to each person, and why the few can win a rule that costs the many.

Being better at making everything is not the same as making everything at a lower cost. A person’s cost of a good is what he gives up to make it, so a person who is worse at everything can still be the cheaper maker of something. That is comparative advantage. When the good is worth more to the buyer than it costs the seller, both can gain; the price only decides how they split the gain. A cheaper newcomer makes the total gain bigger, but the old seller loses, and he has a reason to ask the government to keep the newcomer out.

The four-step diagnostic

Use these steps whenever a question asks who should make a good, what price two people can trade at, or who gains and who loses when a seller enters or is kept out.

  1. Compare costs, not abilities.
    Who can make more of everything tells you who has the absolute advantage. Who gives up less for the next unit tells you who has the comparative advantage. Baker gives up 0.40 for his first cake; Adam gives up 0.90.
  2. Find the range of prices.
    A trade helps both people only at a price above the seller’s cost and below the buyer’s worth. For the third cake, that is any price from 0.80 to 0.95.
  3. Add up the gains, then split them.
    The total gain is the buyer’s worth minus the seller’s cost (0.95 − 0.80 = 0.15). The price only decides how much goes to each side.
  4. Name each person who gains and each who loses.
    When a cheaper seller enters, total gains rise, but the old seller loses. When the newcomer is blocked, the old seller keeps his gain and the buyer and the newcomer pay for it.

What you should be able to do

  1. Tell absolute advantage from comparative advantage, using what each person gives up for one more unit.
  2. Explain why a person who is worse at making everything can still gain from trade.
  3. Find the range of prices at which a trade makes both people better off, and the size of the gain.
  4. Compute each side’s gain (consumer and producer surplus) at a given price.
  5. Show who gains and who loses when a cheaper seller enters, and how much is a transfer.
  6. Explain why a small group of sellers can win a rule that costs many buyers more than it gives the sellers.

Review: what a cake costs Adam · TopHat

Adam stops at two cakes

Adam bakes cakes for himself. The ingredients in a cake could have made bread, so each cake costs him the bread he gives up. With no cakes he makes $13.50 of other goods. His first cake costs 0.90, his second 1.00, and his third 1.10, because as he makes less bread, each loaf he gives up is worth more to him. Total cost is the sum of the marginal costs: two cakes cost 0.90 + 1.00 = 1.90, an average of 0.95. Class also reviewed why marginal cost rises when cooks are added to a fixed kitchen: each extra cook adds less output.

TopHat check. Adam’s first cake is worth 1.15 to him and costs 0.90; the second is worth 1.05 and costs 1.00; the third is worth 0.95 and costs 1.10. How many does he bake? Most of the class got it right: two. The third cake would leave him 0.15 worse off. Economists assume people act in what they see as their own interest, even when others think they eat too much cake.

Baker: worse at everything, cheaper at cakes

Absolute and comparative advantage

In a day, Adam can make 10 cakes or $13.50 of other goods. Baker can make only 8 cakes or $8.80 of other goods. Adam can make more of both, so he has the absolute advantage in both goods. Does that mean Adam should make everything himself? No.

Look at what each man gives up for the next cake. Baker gives up 0.40 for his first cake and 0.60 for his second; Adam gives up 0.90 and 1.00. Baker gives up less for each of cakes 1 to 5, and their costs are equal at the sixth (1.40). So Baker has the comparative advantage in cakes: he is much worse at other goods, so a cake takes little from him. Adam does better to make other goods and trade some of them to Baker for cakes. Everybody has a comparative advantage in something.

A student asked whether this is how countries trade. The instructor’s answer: the same logic works at the country level, but countries do not really trade; people in one country buy from people in another.

Not on the slides: AI and the brain surgeon

Why would you not want a brain surgeon at the cash register at McDonald’s? Every hour he spends at the register is an hour of surgery he does not do. The better he gets at surgery, the more it costs for him to do anything else. The instructor applied the same logic to AI: the better AI gets at one task, such as coding, the more it costs to have it do another, such as checking email. Even if AI were better at every mental task, it would not follow that it should do all of them.

The third cake: a trade that suits both men · TopHat

Any price from 0.80 to 0.95

Alone, each man stops at two cakes. Adam’s third cake is worth 0.95 to him but costs him 1.10 to bake. Baker’s third cake costs him only 0.80 (and is worth only 0.70 to Baker himself). So Baker can bake a third cake and sell it to Adam. Adam pays at most 0.95; Baker takes at least 0.80. There is 0.15 of gain to share. This is why the course taught exchange before production: the refugee camp showed trade with no production at all. Now trade comes with specialization in production.

With only two traders, we cannot say exactly where the price lands. Economists call this a bilateral monopoly: a good bargainer on either side pulls the price toward his end of the range. In an aside, the instructor noted that bakers do use middlemen, such as the suppliers who bring flour and eggs from farms, but most of us buy straight from the bakery.

TopHat check. At which price do both men end up better off: 0.70, 0.90, 1.00, or 1.10? Most of the class chose 0.90. Baker would refuse 0.70, which is below his cost. Adam would refuse 1.00 or 1.10, which is more than the cake is worth to him, even though it is less than or equal to his own cost of baking it. Any price in the range works, such as 0.85 or 0.91.

The price splits the gain

At 0.90, Adam’s consumer surplus is 0.95 − 0.90 = 0.05 and Baker’s producer surplus is 0.90 − 0.80 = 0.10. At 0.85, the split flips: Adam gets 0.10 and Baker 0.05. The total stays 0.15. Baker wants the price high and Adam wants it low; the price decides only who gets the gain.

Carter, the cheaper newcomer · TopHat

Who gains and who loses?

A third producer, Carter, can bake the third cake for 0.60. He offers it to Adam for 0.70 instead of Baker’s 0.90. A cake is a cake, so Adam switches. Carter could charge anything up to 0.79 and still take the sale from Baker, and he would not go below his cost of 0.60.

TopHat check. Which statement describes this example? The answer: Adam and Carter gain, and Baker loses his gain from the sale. “All three gain, because trade is voluntary” is wrong: each trade is voluntary, but Baker is worse off.

The total gain rises by 0.20, which is what the economy saves: Baker’s cake used up 0.80 of other goods, Carter’s uses only 0.60, and the freed resources can make more bread. Adam gains 0.20 more, Carter gains 0.10, and Baker loses 0.10. Of Adam’s extra 0.20, 0.10 is a transfer from Baker. Baker’s loss is real for him, but it does not by itself mean Carter should be kept out. In principle, the winners could pay Baker 0.10 and still come out ahead. The trouble, as with the holdout problem from an earlier class, is that Baker has a reason to overstate his loss.

In an aside, the instructor asked whether Carter and Adam would know what Baker charges. How much anyone learns depends on the costs and benefits of learning it. Buyers and sellers in a market gain directly from being informed, so they tend to be well informed. A voter’s choice in politics does not pay off for him in the same way.

Blocking Carter: the few against the many

Blocking entry costs 0.20

Suppose Baker persuades the authorities to bar Carter: Carter is an outsider, he might put rat poison in the cakes, so only licensed bakers may sell. The price returns to 0.90 and the total gain falls from 0.35 to 0.15. Unless the rule really buys safety or quality, that 0.20 is a net loss. A license fee that raises Carter’s cost to Baker’s level does the same thing. Would a baker really sell poisoned cakes for long? Customers who got sick would not come back.

The slide’s example: suppose a state lets you braid hair only after hundreds of hours of licensed training, and grants a license only when existing salons are judged inadequate. The incumbents are few and each has a large stake. The buyers are many and each has a small stake. The few find it easier to organize, so they often win, though not always.

Not on the slides: concentrated benefits, dispersed costs

Sugar. The United States limits sugar imports with a tariff-rate quota: each year a set amount of foreign sugar comes in at a low tariff, and sugar above that amount pays a much higher tariff, so little of it comes in. That raises the U.S. price of sugar. The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) reports estimates that the sugar program costs U.S. consumers, including food makers, $2.5 billion to $3.5 billion a year: about $7 to $10 for each person. No one person would spend more than that to end the program, while sugar producers gain $1.4 billion to $2.7 billion a year and lobby both parties. The instructor gave one sign of the quotas: Coke made in Mexico uses cane sugar, while U.S. Coke uses high-fructose corn syrup. (This paragraph corrects two points from class: sugar above the quota is not banned but pays a high tariff, and the cost per person is about $7 to $10, not $5. Source: GAO-24-106144, October 2023.)

Bars. In the town where the instructor tended bar, a new bar needed the approval of the existing bars before it could get a liquor license. Boulder, Colorado, capped the number of licenses, so a new bar could open only when an old one sold its license.

Cartels. Sellers can also try to agree to restrict output and raise the price. Each member gains by cheating and selling more, just as each member of a group project gains by letting the others do the work. The instructor’s example: a small OPEC member, such as Kuwait, can pump more than its quota without moving the world price much. Without a barrier to entry, the high profits also draw in new sellers.

Why firms lobby. If lobbying for protection raises a public company’s profits, a CEO who refuses keeps the share price low, and the board or an outside buyer can replace him with someone who will lobby. Profit can come from serving customers better, like Carter, or from keeping rivals out, like Baker. The instructor’s summary: be pro-market, not necessarily pro-business. New ways of doing things, such as Uber and Lyft against the taxi rules, are one way around entry barriers, though once they led the market they too wanted less competition.

A student asked whether buyers could organize too. They can, and when they do, they push back. But with 10,000 buyers, each one hopes the others will do the work: group projects all the way down. George Stigler won a Nobel Prize partly for showing how regulation can serve the regulated industry; his student Sam Peltzman showed that the price in a regulated market depends on how strong each side is politically.

Check your reasoning

Answer before you open each one. Every question uses only material from class, with new numbers.

Question 1 — In a day, Priya can make 12 shirts or $30 of other goods. Sam can make 6 shirts or $9 of other goods. Each person’s cost of a shirt stays the same however many shirts they make. Who has the absolute advantage in each good? Who has the comparative advantage in shirts?

Priya can make more of both, so she has the absolute advantage in both goods. A shirt costs Priya $30 ÷ 12 = $2.50 of other goods, but it costs Sam only $9 ÷ 6 = $1.50. So Sam has the comparative advantage in shirts, and Priya in other goods.

Question 2 — A bookshelf is worth $48 to Lena. Building it herself would cost her $55, so she skips it. Ravi can build it for $36. What range of prices makes both better off, and how large is the total gain?

Any price above Ravi’s cost and below Lena’s worth: from $36 to $48. The total gain is $48 − $36 = $12, whatever the price.

Question 3 — In Question 2, which of these prices leaves both better off: $34, $45, or $50? A friend says, “Any price below $55 is good for Lena, because that is what it would cost her.” Is the friend right?

Only $45. At $34, Ravi loses. At $50, Lena pays more than the shelf is worth to her. The friend is wrong: Lena would never build it herself, so $55 is not the limit. The limit is what the shelf is worth to her, $48.

Question 4 — In Question 2, find each person’s gain at a price of $40 and at $44.

At $40: Ravi gains $40 − $36 = $4 and Lena gains $48 − $40 = $8. At $44: Ravi gains $8 and Lena $4. The total is $12 both times; the price only splits it.

Question 5 — Lena has been buying from Ravi at $40. Tomas can build the same shelf for $30, and he offers it for $33. Who gains, who loses, and by how much? What happens to the total gain?

Lena’s gain rises from $8 to $48 − $33 = $15 (+$7). Tomas gains $33 − $30 = $3. Ravi loses his $4. The total rises from $12 to $18, up $6, which is the resources saved: Ravi’s shelf used up $36, Tomas’s uses $30. Of Lena’s extra $7, $4 is a transfer from Ravi.

Question 6 — Ravi persuades the town to require a builder’s license that Tomas cannot get, and the price goes back to $40. What is lost, and who bears it?

The total gain falls from $18 back to $12, so $6 is lost, unless the license really buys safety or quality. Ravi gets back his $4. Lena loses $7 and Tomas loses $3.

Question 7 — A rule keeps new sellers out of a market. The four sellers already in it each gain $2 million a year. Each of 20 million buyers pays $1.50 more a year. Compare the totals, and explain why the rule might survive.

The sellers gain $8 million in all; the buyers lose $30 million. The rule might still survive because each seller would spend up to $2 million to keep it, while no buyer would spend more than $1.50 to end it. Four sellers can organize; 20 million buyers each hope someone else will do it.

Question 8 — A lawyer types twice as fast as her assistant. An hour of her legal work earns $300. Typing a brief would take her one hour or her assistant two hours, and the assistant is paid $25 an hour. Who should type the brief?

The assistant. The lawyer has the absolute advantage in typing, but an hour of her typing costs $300 of legal work. The assistant’s two hours cost $50. Like the brain surgeon at the register, the lawyer’s skill at law is what makes her typing expensive.

Bottom line

Cost is what you give up, so the person who is worse at everything can still be the cheaper maker of something. When a good is worth more to the buyer than it costs the seller, both can gain at any price between the two, and the price only splits the gain. A cheaper newcomer raises the total gain but takes the old seller’s gain. That is why the old seller may ask the government to keep the newcomer out, and why the few who gain a lot can beat the many who each lose a little.
