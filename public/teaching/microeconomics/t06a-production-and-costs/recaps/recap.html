What Does It Cost? Marginal Analysis, Production, and Costs

Where to put the next hour of a fixed resource, what a cost really is, why the marginal pulls the average, why marginal cost rises, and when a producer should stop.

The cost of making something is the best thing you give up to make it. Every resource has other uses: the flour in a cake could have made bread. Marginal cost is the extra cost of one more unit; total cost is the sum of the marginal costs, and average cost is total cost divided by the number of units. Marginal cost rises as you produce more, because you give up things you value more and because adding workers to a fixed kitchen adds less and less output. Every one of these decisions is made at the margin: put the next hour where it returns the most, and keep producing while the next unit is worth more than it costs.

The four-step diagnostic

Use these steps whenever a question asks what something costs, how to split a resource, or how much to produce.

  1. Name what is given up.
    A cost is the value of the best alternative use of the resources. When Adam bakes his first cake, his other goods fall from $13.50 to $12.60, so the cake costs him 0.90.
  2. Build the marginal column.
    Marginal cost is the change in total cost from one more unit. Total cost is the sum of the marginal costs, and average cost is total cost divided by the number of units.
  3. Compare at the margin, not by averages or totals.
    Give the next hour to whichever use returns more for that hour. When the marginal is above the average, it pulls the average up; when it is below, it pulls the average down.
  4. Stop where the next unit is no longer worth its cost.
    Keep going while the next unit’s worth is greater than its marginal cost. Adam bakes two cakes, because the third costs 1.10 and is worth only 0.95 to him.

What you should be able to do

  1. Split a fixed resource between two uses by comparing the return of the next unit in each.
  2. State the cost of a good as the value of what is given up to make it.
  3. Build marginal, total, and average cost from a table, and update an average when one more unit is added.
  4. Explain why marginal cost rises, using diminishing marginal returns and resources suited to different uses.
  5. Find how many units a producer makes by comparing marginal worth with marginal cost.

The lecture this time

This part of class started a new deck on production and cost. It covered the first four parts of the deck in order.

Warm-up: which factory gets the next hour?

Put each hour where it returns more

A producer has ten machine-hours to split between factory A and factory B (the slide calls them lines). A’s hours return $20, $18, $16, $14, $12, and so on; B’s return $15, $14.50, $14, $13.50, $13. Each added hour returns less than the one before. Economists call this diminishing marginal productivity.

Splitting the hours five and five returns $150. Can the producer do better? The class went hour by hour, asking which factory the next hour should go to. A student caught a slip in the order, and the instructor corrected it: the first three hours go to A ($20, $18, $16); the fourth goes to B, because B’s first hour returns $15 and A’s fourth only $14; the next goes to B ($14.50); then A’s fourth hour and B’s third tie at $14, and so on. The best ten hours are four on A and six on B, for $150.50, 50 cents more than five and five. All ten on A return only $110, and all ten on B $127.50.

Even with two hours, the rule matters: one hour on each factory returns $20 + $15 = $35, but both on A return $20 + $18 = $38. The lesson: do not split evenly or pick the bigger factory. Give each hour to the use with the larger return for that hour.

What a cake costs Adam · TopHat

A cake costs Adam the Y he gives up

Adam lives alone and bakes cakes for himself. The flour, sugar, and eggs in a cake could make other things, such as bread. We count everything else he could make in dollars and call it Y. With no cakes, Adam makes $13.50 of other goods. One cake leaves $12.60, so the first cake costs him 0.90. With one cake, his total cost and average cost are also 0.90.

A second cake costs 1.00, so his total cost is 0.90 + 1.00 = 1.90 and his average cost is 1.90 ÷ 2 = 0.95. Total cost is always the sum of the marginal costs. Why does each cake cost more? The instructor’s explanation: each cake means one less loaf of bread. As Adam has less bread, each loaf is worth more to him, so the bread he gives up for the next cake is worth more.

The class worked out the fifth cake together. The first four cost 4.20 in all, and the fifth costs 1.30, so the total is 5.50 and the average is 5.50 ÷ 5 = 1.10. His other goods fall from 9.30 to 8.00. Every resource has other uses: an hour at one job is an hour not spent at another, or on the couch. A farmer who plants an acre of wheat cannot plant soybeans on it.

TopHat check. Adam’s average cost of four cakes is 1.05, and the fifth costs 1.30. What is his new average? Most of the class got the answer: 1.10. Add the fifth cake’s cost to the total of the first four, 4.20 + 1.30 = 5.50, then divide by five.

The marginal pulls the average

Add up the net worth of everyone in the classroom and divide by the number of people: that is the average. Now Elon Musk walks in. His net worth is far above the average, so the average jumps. Whenever the marginal is above the average, it pulls the average up; whenever it is below, it pulls the average down. Adam’s fifth cake costs 1.30, above his average of 1.05, so his average rises, but only to 1.10. The difference between marginal and average cost will matter again when the course reaches competitive firms.

Why marginal cost rises

Adding workers to a fixed kitchen

The bread story is one reason. A second reason is diminishing marginal returns. With no cooks, a kitchen makes nothing. The first cook adds output; a second may add even more, because one can work the flat-top while the other works the grill. But keep adding cooks to the same kitchen and each one adds less, because each has less equipment to work with. The instructor saw the same thing as a bartender: pack more bartenders behind a bar with the same taps and ice wells, and they start running into one another.

Marginal product is the extra output from one more worker. On the slide, each worker costs 12 Y. The fifth worker adds 4 units, so each added unit costs 12 ÷ 4 = 3 Y. The sixth adds only 2, so each added unit costs 12 ÷ 2 = 6 Y. Across the six workers, the cost per added unit runs 2.00, 1.20, 1.50, 2.00, 3.00, 6.00: it falls while marginal product is still rising, then rises once marginal product falls.

Resources suited to different uses

A third reason: resources differ. A farmer who wants more wheat plants it first on the land best suited to wheat, which is poor for soybeans, so little is given up. Each further acre of wheat goes on land better suited to soybeans, so each extra bushel of wheat costs more soybeans. Rising marginal cost comes from both: resources that differ, and fixed inputs that get crowded.

How many cakes does Adam bake?

Adam stops at two cakes

On the chart, bars show what each cake costs Adam to bake, and dots show what each cake is worth to him as the eater, the most Y he would give up for it. The first cake is worth 1.15 and costs 0.90, so he bakes it. The second is worth 1.05 and costs 1.00, so he bakes it too. The third costs 1.10 but is worth only 0.95, so he stops at two. The class worked this one aloud instead of as a TopHat poll.

The rule: keep doing something while its marginal benefit is greater than its marginal cost, and stop when it is not. The instructor called it “a very good rule for life.”

Check your reasoning

Answer before you open each one. Every question uses only material from class, with new numbers.

Question 1 — A bakery has six oven-hours a day to split between bread and pastries. The next hour on bread returns $40, $30, $20, $12, $6, $3; on pastries $27, $25, $23, $21, $19, $17. How should it split the six hours?

Take the six largest returns, one hour at a time: $40 and $30 on bread, then $27, $25, $23, and $21 on pastries. So two hours on bread and four on pastries, for $166. Three and three returns $165; all six on pastries $132; all six on bread $111. Every hour used ($21 or more) returns more than every hour left over ($20 or less).

Question 2 — A tutor’s average cost over six sessions is $20. The seventh session costs $34. What is the new average cost?

Total cost was 6 × $20 = $120; add $34 to get $154; divide by 7: $22. The seventh session costs more than the old average, so it pulls the average up, but only part of the way.

Question 3 — A firm’s average cost over four units is $15. The fifth unit costs $10. What happens to the average?

Total cost goes from $60 to $70, so the average falls to $70 ÷ 5 = $14. A marginal below the average pulls the average down.

Question 4 — Omar makes jam for himself. With no jam, he makes $30.00 of other goods. One jar leaves $28.50, two leave $26.70, and three leave $24.60. Find the marginal, total, and average cost of each jar.

Marginal cost: 1.50, 1.80, 2.10 (each drop in other goods). Total cost: 1.50, 3.30, 5.40 (the running sum). Average cost: 1.50, 1.65, 1.80 (total divided by jars).

Question 5 — A car wash has a fixed number of bays. Each worker costs $60 a day. Workers one to five add 10, 15, 12, 8, and 5 cars a day. What does each added car cost with each worker? Why does it first fall?

Divide $60 by each marginal product: $6, $4, $5, $7.50, $12 per added car. It falls at the second worker because marginal product rises from 10 to 15 (two workers use the bays better). After that, marginal product falls, so each added car costs more: diminishing marginal returns.

Question 6 — In Question 4, a first jar of jam is worth 2.40 to Omar, a second 2.00, and a third 1.70. How many jars does he make?

Two. The first gains him 2.40 − 1.50 = 0.90 and the second 2.00 − 1.80 = 0.20. The third costs 2.10 but is worth only 1.70, a loss of 0.40, so he stops at two jars.

Question 7 — A friend tells Omar: “Your average cost of two jars is 1.65, and a third jar is worth 1.70 to you, so make it.” Is the friend right?

No. The friend compares the third jar’s worth with the average cost. The third jar costs its marginal cost, 2.10, which is more than its worth, 1.70. Making it would leave Omar 0.40 worse off.

Question 8 — A farmer can grow corn or cotton. Some of his fields suit corn, some suit cotton. As he plants more corn, why does each extra bushel of corn cost him more cotton?

He plants corn first on the fields best for corn and worst for cotton, so little cotton is given up. More corn has to go on fields better and better suited to cotton, so each extra bushel of corn gives up more cotton. Marginal cost rises because the resources differ.

Bottom line

A cost is what you give up. Build marginal cost from the change in what is given up; add the marginals for the total and divide for the average. A marginal above the average pulls it up. Marginal cost rises because each unit takes resources that are worth more elsewhere and because fixed inputs get crowded. Put each unit of a resource where its next return is highest, and produce while the next unit is worth more than it costs.
