Public Goods and the Commons

Why some goods are worth making but hard to sell, how people find ways to pay for them anyway, and why a lake that anyone may fish gets fished too hard.

Class meeting 15 · October 15, 2026 · 10-minute review · The first 38 minutes of class

Markets work best when a seller can keep out people who do not pay. Some goods fail that test. Once a fireworks show starts or a radio signal goes out, everyone nearby gets it, so few people will pay for it. A good can be worth making, because the values of everyone who uses it add up to more than its cost, and still not get made, because each person hopes the others will pay. People find ways around this: taxes, patents and copyrights, advertising, and subscriptions. A resource that anyone may use but that runs out, like the fish in a lake, has the opposite problem: it gets used too fast.

The four-step diagnostic

Use these steps whenever a question asks what kind of good something is, whether it is worth making, or why it is overused.

  1. Ask whether the good is rival.
    Does one person’s use leave less for others? A laptop in use by one person cannot be used by another, so it is rival. Many people can watch the same fireworks show, so the show is nonrival.
  2. Ask whether non-payers can be kept out.
    Can the seller exclude people who do not pay, at a reasonable cost? A pool owner can put up a fence. No one can stop a neighbor from watching fireworks in the sky.
  3. If the good is nonrival, add everyone’s value.
    Everyone uses the same unit, so add each person’s value for that unit and compare the sum with the cost. In class, three viewers valued an episode at $5, $3, and $2. The sum, $10, beats the $8 cost, though no single viewer values it at $8.
  4. Ask how it gets paid for, or how its use gets limited.
    For a public good: taxes, intellectual property, advertising, or a way to exclude (a subscription). For a resource anyone may use: an owner, licenses with enforcement, or a user group that sets and polices quotas.

What you should be able to do

  1. Sort a good into private, club, common-pool, or pure public, using rivalry and excludability.
  2. Decide whether a public good is worth making by adding everyone’s value at one quantity.
  3. Explain why a public good that is worth making may still not get made (the free-rider problem).
  4. Name ways people pay for public goods, and what each one gives up.
  5. Explain why a resource that anyone may use gets used too fast, and how rules or owners can slow it.

Finishing the permit market

The cap fixes the total; trade moves the credits

Class picked up the paint maker and the refiner, with 20 of the 40 credits each. One more credit is worth $2.87 to the paint maker and $1.10 to the refiner, so they are not at an equilibrium. The refiner sells to the paint maker, but it cannot charge more than the market price of $2.20, because the paint maker can buy there instead. They trade until each one’s value of one more credit equals $2.20: the paint maker ends with 30 and the refiner with 10. Total emissions stay at 40.

A student had asked last time why the ocean-view bargain did not look like supply and demand. There was no market for views, only one builder and one group of tenants. Here, a market price for credits exists, and both firms can look at it.

Economists think there is an optimal amount of pollution, and it is not zero. A cap plus trade gets the credits to the firms that value them most. Whether the political process picks the right cap is a separate question.

Externality problems are property-rights problems

No one owns the air or the ocean, so no one has a reason to protect them. The same logic explains why chickens, pigs, and cows are not at risk of extinction while rhinos and elephants are. People own the farm animals and profit from them. Some communities near wildlife now fence off reserves and post guards against poachers, because tourists will pay to see the animals. In effect, they are creating property rights in the animals.

Four kinds of goods

Two tests: rivalry and excludability

A private good is rival and excludable: a student’s laptop. Most of economics is about private goods. A club good is nonrival, up to crowding, and excludable: a swimming pool with a fence and a fee, or a golf course. A common-pool resource is rival but hard to exclude people from: a rhino’s horn or an elephant’s ivory in an open forest, or the fish in the ocean. A pure public good is nonrival and nonexcludable: a fireworks show, or an idea such as the Pythagorean theorem.

Property rights in ideas

Once an idea exists, anyone can use it, so people would have little reason to pay for it. Intellectual property creates rights in ideas: trademarks, patents, trade secrets, and copyright for books, music, and films. A patent makes the invention public in exchange for the right; a trade secret keeps it hidden. That is one reason a firm such as Coca-Cola can prefer to keep its recipe secret rather than patent it.

The trade-off: these rights give people a reason to create new ideas, but they also give the creator a temporary monopoly. That is why patents and copyrights expire.

Government, roads, and licenses

Governments often provide fireworks shows and pay for them with taxes. National defense is the classic case: the deterrence the military provides protects everyone at once, and it cannot leave out a household that did not pay. Even defense is not always a pure public good: if the country had to fight on two coasts at once, it would have to choose which to defend.

Roads are often called public goods, but a busy road is rival: two cars cannot fill the same space. Tolls show that some roads can exclude non-payers. So a road is closer to a club good or a common-pool resource, depending on how crowded it is.

Hunting and fishing licenses are one way to limit the use of a common-pool resource. Colorado issues elk licenses by lottery and says where each hunter may go: the herds are not stable, so wildlife officials estimate how many animals can be taken each year. Texas pays hunters to kill feral hogs, which multiply fast and damage crops, so there is no reason to ration them. Licenses work only with enforcement: without rangers checking, no one would buy one. Fishing groups can also claim an area, assign each member a quota, and keep outsiders out.

Public goods and free riders · TopHat

Is an $8 program worth making?

Three viewers would pay $5, $3, and $2 for one more episode of a program broadcast over the air, and making it costs $8. Anyone with an antenna can watch, so no one can be charged. Without a way to charge, the episode never gets made.

Yet radio and TV stations exist. They sell advertising, which is rival and excludable: two ads cannot run in the same 30 seconds, and a station can refuse an advertiser who does not pay. The music attracts listeners, and the listeners’ attention is an input the station sells. A satellite service such as Sirius XM takes another route: it turns what would be a public good into a private good that it can sell.

TopHat check. Is the episode worth making? Yes: everyone watches the same episode, so we add the values, and $5 + $3 + $2 = $10, more than $8. The instructor walked through the wrong options. “Every viewer values it above zero” is not enough: if three viewers valued it at $1 each, $3 would not cover $8. The average value, about $3.33, is not the test, and neither is whether any one viewer would pay $8.

One free rider sinks the program

Suppose the episode is made only if pledges cover its $8 cost. Viewer B hopes the others will pay and says it is worth $0. The pledges add to $5 + $0 + $2 = $7, less than $8, so nothing is made, and B’s free ride fails. Each viewer is tempted to hold back, yet together they value the episode at $10. That is the free-rider problem.

Group projects work the same way. Everyone in the group gets the same grade, whatever each person puts in, so the grade is in a sense unowned. Each member wants the others to work hard, because that raises the grade, but would rather put in little effort personally. National defense has the same structure: each of us benefits, but each would rather the others pay.

The commons

Why the lake gets fished too hard

Anyone may fish the lake, and no rule reserves the catch. Each fisher keeps the fish he catches, while much of the cost of a smaller stock falls on the other fishers. So everyone fishes sooner and harder, with more boats and hours than the extra catch is worth.

That is the theory, but people often cooperate more than it predicts. Elinor Ostrom, the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics (2009), studied how local communities manage resources such as forests, fisheries, and pastures, and found that user groups sometimes manage them well. Her Nobel lecture is online: search for “Elinor Ostrom Nobel lecture.”

Check your reasoning

Answer before you open each one. Every question uses only material from class, with new numbers.

Question 1 — Sort each good: a slice of pizza; wild mushrooms in a large public forest; a neighborhood tornado siren; a members-only gym that is rarely crowded.

The pizza is a private good: rival and easy to exclude. The mushrooms are a common-pool resource: one person’s pick leaves fewer for others, but keeping pickers out of a large forest is costly. The siren is a pure public good: everyone nearby hears it, and no one can be kept from hearing it. The gym is a club good: nonrival until it gets crowded, and the owner can keep non-members out.

Question 2 — Spraying a block for mosquitoes protects every house on it. Four households value it at $40, $25, $20, and $15, and it costs $90. Is it worth doing?

Yes. Everyone gets the same spraying, so add the values: $40 + $25 + $20 + $15 = $100, more than $90. No household values it at $90 on its own; the sum is what counts.

Question 3 — In Question 2, the spraying happens only if pledges cover the $90. The household that values it at $25 pledges $0, hoping the others will pay. What happens?

The pledges add to $40 + $0 + $20 + $15 = $75, less than $90, so the spraying does not happen, though the block values it at $100. That is the free-rider problem: the household that held back loses the spraying too.

Question 4 — Five neighbors each value a new streetlight at $12, and it costs $50. One neighbor says, “No one would pay $50, and the average value is only $12, so it is not worth it.” Is that right?

No. The streetlight is nonrival, so add the values: 5 × $12 = $60, more than $50. Neither the largest single value nor the average is the test.

Question 5 — A podcast is free to listen to. How can it pay its costs? What changes if it moves behind a paywall?

Free, it can sell advertising: the listeners’ attention is an input it sells to advertisers, which is rival and excludable. Behind a paywall, non-payers are kept out, so the podcast becomes excludable, like a club good, and listeners become the customers.

Question 6 — A drug company holds a patent on a new drug. What does the patent do for the company, what does it cost everyone else, and why does it expire?

The patent lets the company charge for its idea, which gives it a reason to spend on research. It also gives the company a temporary monopoly, so the drug costs more while the patent lasts. Patents expire so that, after a time, anyone may copy the drug. The patent also makes the formula public, which is why some firms keep a trade secret instead.

Question 7 — Eight boats fish an open lake. A ninth boat would catch $500 of fish a day and cost its owner $400 a day to run. With a smaller stock, each of the other eight boats would catch $25 less a day. Does the owner send the boat? Should the boat go out?

The owner sends it: he gains $500 − $400 = $100 a day. But the other boats lose 8 × $25 = $200 a day, a cost the owner does not bear. In all, the ninth boat makes the people who fish the lake $100 a day worse off. That is the commons problem.

Question 8 — A state sells fishing licenses for a lake but hires no wardens. What happens?

Few people buy licenses, because no one checks, and the lake is still fished too hard. A license limits use only if someone enforces it.

Question 9 — A highway is empty at 3 a.m. and jammed at 5 p.m. Is it a public good?

Not a pure one. At 3 a.m. one more car takes nothing from anyone, so it is nonrival. At 5 p.m. each car slows the others, so it is rival. Tolls can keep non-payers out, which would make it excludable. It is closer to a club good when empty and tolled, and to a common-pool resource when jammed and free.

Question 10 — In a four-person group project, everyone gets the same grade. Why might the project go badly, and what change would help?

Each member gains when the others work hard but bears the full cost of his own effort, so each is tempted to put in little: a free-rider problem. Tying each person’s grade to his own work, for example with peer evaluations, makes each member bear the result of his effort.

Bottom line

Two tests sort goods: rivalry and excludability. A pure public good can be worth making, because everyone’s values add up, yet go unmade, because each person hopes the others will pay. People pay for such goods through taxes, intellectual property, advertising, and ways to exclude, and each route gives something up. A resource anyone may use gets used too fast; owners, enforced licenses, and user groups can slow it. Next, Lecture 10 turns to production and cost.
