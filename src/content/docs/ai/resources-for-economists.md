:::note
Reference — for when you're up and running. You don't need any of this to get
started; it's here for after, when you want to go deeper.
:::

This guide is a starting point, not the last word. Below is a small, curated set of
further reading and tools worth knowing about as you go deeper — chosen because each
is genuinely useful to an academic economist, not because it's popular. The list is
deliberately short; a few good resources beat a wall of links.

:::caution
External tools and pages change — versions update, projects move, licenses shift.
Treat every link here as a *lead to verify*, not a settled fact: confirm a tool's
current behavior, maintenance, and license against its own page before you rely on
it for real teaching or research. This is the same verify-from-the-source
discipline the rest of this guide insists on.
:::

## Official documentation

- **[Claude Code documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/overview)** —
  Anthropic's own reference. Comprehensive and developer-facing; when you want the
  authoritative answer on a feature, a command, or how something behaves, this is
  the source. Many pages in this guide link to specific sections of it.

## Understanding the landscape

- **[Korinek, "Generative AI for Economic Research"
  (JEL, 2023)](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/generative-ai-for-economic-research-use-cases-and-implications-for-economists/)**
  — the foundational survey of how generative AI fits into economic research,
  organized around concrete use cases (ideation, writing, background research, data
  analysis, coding, math). A good map of *where* these tools help, from an
  economist writing for economists. (The link is the public Brookings version of
  the published article.)

## Tools and collections

- **[Scott Cunningham's MixtapeTools](https://github.com/scunning1975/MixtapeTools)**
  — a collection of AI-assisted research tools from an applied economist, including
  a "Referee 2" empirical-code audit protocol and a "Blindspot" review pass. Most
  relevant once you're using Claude on real analysis and want a structured *second
  check* on your work, in the spirit of [Verifying Claude's
  Work](/ai/verifying-claudes-work/).
- **[awesome-ai-for-economists](https://github.com/hanlulong/awesome-ai-for-economists)**
  — a community-curated list of AI tools, libraries, and resources aimed at
  economics research, teaching, and policy. A browsing list to scan when you're
  looking for something specific; quality varies across a list like this, so apply
  the verify-before-you-rely rule to anything you pick up from it.

## Reference management

- **Zotero, connected to Claude** — several community projects (for example,
  [zotero-mcp](https://github.com/54yyyu/zotero-mcp)) let Claude search and cite
  from your [Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/) library through the Model Context
  Protocol. The appeal for our purposes is integrity: pulling *real, already-saved*
  references from your own library is the antidote to Claude inventing
  citations — the cardinal sin from the [integrity
  page](/ai/trust-safety-integrity/). These are third-party tools; vet the one
  you choose before connecting it to your library.

## A peer guide

- **[Chris Blattman, "AI for Professionals Who Don't
  Code"](https://claudeblattman.com/)** — a political economist's freely-shared
  collection of AI workflows and reusable "skills," aimed at non-coding academics.
  Broader and more tooling-heavy than this guide, and a useful complement once
  you've got the fundamentals here. Different emphasis, overlapping audience.

---

If you find a resource that belongs here, the bar is simple: it has to be genuinely
useful to a working economist, and you have to be able to verify it's real and
maintained. That second test is the whole spirit of this guide — applied to the
reading list itself.
