# Markets, Myths, and Political Economy

This is the last topic of the course, so I am not going to teach you anything brand new today. I want instead to hand the toolkit back to you and watch you use it. All term we built a way of reasoning: scarcity and competition, cost and trade-offs, property and exchange, the discipline of tracing cause and effect, and the line between what is and what ought to be. Today we turn that reasoning on the claims people actually make about markets. Some are myths that fall apart the moment you apply a tool you already own; others are real questions about where markets fall short and whether politics does any better.

One thread runs through the whole hour. Scarcity makes competition unavoidable; we established that on the first day. The payoff we did not fully draw out then is this: suppress competition in one form and it does not disappear, it reappears in another, usually worse. That single idea unlocks both the market myths and the political ones. Keep one distinction handy throughout: a *positive* claim is about what is, the kind economics can test, while a *normative* one is about what ought to be and rests on values economics cannot supply. Most of today's confusions come from smuggling the second past you dressed as the first.

## A Market Is Not a Thing, It Is People Trading

Start with the myth buried in the word itself. We say "the market" as if it were a machine humming in the basement, an impersonal force that crushes people and must be reined in. But a market is not a thing at all. It is just people, you and me and millions of strangers, agreeing to trade on terms we each find acceptable, which makes it exactly as personal as the people in it.

This matters more than it sounds. Once you treat "the market" as a faceless mechanism, it becomes easy to argue that someone should step in and rescue people from its "dictates." But look at what that rescue involves. When a third party blocks two people from making a deal they both wanted, those two are not freed from anything; they are subjected to the dictates of the third party instead. The choice is never between the impersonal market and freedom. It is between the choices people make for themselves and the choices someone else makes for them, and a great deal of policy talk dresses up the second as the first. Restrict people's freedom to trade and you have replaced the judgments of the people directly involved with the judgment of whoever now holds the power.

## The "Same" Thing at Different Prices Is Rarely the Same Thing

Here is a complaint you have surely heard: the very same product sells for wildly different prices in different stores, which supposedly proves that supply and demand is a fairy tale and somebody is gouging us. The complaint rests on a sleight of hand, calling two things "the same" when they are not.

Two cans of soup with identical labels are physically identical, yes. But the conditions wrapped around them differ, and you are buying those conditions too. One store sits on expensive downtown land; the other where land is cheap, and that cost difference has to be recovered somewhere. One keeps most of its inventory in stock so you finish your list in one trip and gets you through checkout fast; the other keeps less and leaves you in line. Sowell reports a consumer magazine study in northern California that priced an identical basket of brand-name groceries across several stores: as he tells it, the bill ran from about eighty dollars at the cheapest to roughly a hundred and twenty-five at the most expensive. The cheap store carried far less of the list and was slow at checkout; the pricey one sat on some of the costliest real estate in the country.

So the shoppers were not paying different prices for the same thing. They were paying different prices for different things: a fast trip versus a slow one, a complete list versus a partial one, convenient location versus cheap rent. The "same product, different price" complaint ignores everything but the label.

A close cousin is the demand for "reasonable" or "affordable" prices. To insist that a price be reasonable is to insist that reality bend to our budget, but the resources it takes to build an apartment, train a doctor, or grow food do not shrink because we would prefer to pay less. Here is the line I most want you to carry out of this room: *prices are not costs; prices are what pay for costs.* When a law forbids charging enough to cover the costs, the costs do not vanish. The supply does. Quantity falls, or quality falls, or both, which is exactly the shortage logic we worked out under price controls. Capping what a hospital may charge does not make it cheaper to build a hospital; it just means longer waits and older equipment.

## Brand Names Sell You Knowledge, Not Illusion

India's first prime minister once asked why a country needs nineteen brands of toothpaste, and the question captures a popular view: brands are a trick, a way to charge more for the same product by spending on advertising. The scarce thing here is knowledge: you cannot personally inspect the refinery that made your gasoline or the kitchen that made your lunch, so a brand economizes on the knowledge you lack.

Picture pulling into a town you have never seen, low on gas and hungry. At an unknown station you have no idea what is in your tank, and at a no-name stand no idea what is in the food. But if the signs say Chevron and McDonald's you stop worrying, because if something goes badly wrong you are dealing with a company worth billions, one you could actually sue and collect from. The brand puts the company's reputation on the line with every sale, forcing it to protect that reputation by protecting you. That is why a brand can be a firm's single most valuable asset; Sowell notes that most of Coca-Cola's market value lies not in its factories or trucks but in the name itself, which a single scandal could destroy.

Even when critics are right that the leading brands "are all alike," the brands still do their job: the question is not whether one soup beats another but whether either would be as safe and consistent if both were sold in anonymous cans with no maker's name. Identify each producer and you force each to answer for quality; hide the producer and the pressure slackens.

You see this most clearly when brands are absent. Before brands took hold in nineteenth-century America, food adulteration was rampant, and it receded as producers like Heinz staked their own names on selling honest goods. In the Soviet Union, where the state allowed no real brands, consumers reportedly taught themselves to read product barcodes to tell which factory had made a thing and steer toward the reliable ones. That brands arise even where the state forbids them shows they serve buyers, not just sellers.

## Non-Profit Does Not Mean Better

Here is a belief that runs deep: profit is a needless surcharge, so an organization that takes no profit must serve people better and cheaper. This is the oldest and most consequential of all the market myths, the picture of a price as a private toll bolted onto a good for someone's gain, with the masses kept from what they need by that toll. If it were true, abolishing the toll would raise everyone's standard of living, and whole nations bet their futures on exactly that bet. The twentieth century ran the experiment: economies that tore out price coordination and profit to end the supposed exploitation did not grow richer, they grew poorer than the economies that kept prices, which is why even committed socialist and communist states brought prices back by the century's end. It was an elementary lesson learned at a terrible cost to hundreds of millions of people. So test the smaller version of the same myth with a tool we have used all term, the feedback of profit and loss.

A for-profit firm has to listen: if it stops serving customers they leave, and if investors stop earning a return they pull their money out. Profit and loss are a relentless stream of feedback that punishes waste and rewards serving people well. A non-profit is, by design, insulated from much of that feedback. Its users often pay less than the full cost of what they get, sometimes nothing, so they cannot discipline it the way paying customers discipline a business, and its donors, especially long-dead ones who left endowments, cannot monitor the spending. When a good is handed out below cost, something other than price still has to do the rationing, because the scarce thing is no less scarce. A church does not auction its pews; it seats people first come, first served, with the occasional reserved seat for a large donor, and queuing, seniority, and personal connections are that rule's usual cousins. Whenever a thing is given away for less than it costs, look for the line, the waiting list, or the well-connected insider, because the scarcity is being settled there instead of at a price. Cut off from that feedback, the people running a non-profit face weak pressure to pursue the organization's stated mission and a freer hand to pursue their own comfort, prestige, or convictions instead. This is the same idea we used in the first week: institutions do not have goals, the people inside them do, so always ask what those people are rewarded and punished for.

The slack shows up in concrete ways. Non-profits often drift from what their founders intended, and the slack leaves room for officials to indulge their prejudices: Sowell points out that for-profit chemical companies hired black chemists in substantial numbers well before non-profit colleges and hospitals would hire black professors and doctors, precisely because the profit-seeking firms paid a price for turning away talent and the non-profits did not.

Now run the market test. If profit really were just an unnecessary markup, non-profits should be able to undercut for-profit firms and slowly replace them, yet the opposite keeps happening. Colleges, which are non-profit, have steadily handed their bookstores and dining halls to outside companies, because those companies run them better and still pay the college more than it earned doing the job itself. Sowell's example is a campus bookstore from which a university rarely cleared a hundred thousand dollars a year; a national chain paid the school half a million a year to run the very same store, and still profited on top. The profit motive was not waste; it was what surfaced the waste the non-profit had been living with.

## Politics Is a Marketplace

Here the course turns toward its political-economy payoff. Government is essential, and I am not waving it away. But "the government" never decides anything; particular officials do, and those officials do not stand above the economy refereeing it. They are players in it, controlling scarce, valuable resources, the use of land, the permission you need to open certain businesses, the contracts and subsidies and licenses people want, and they compete with one another for the support of whoever can keep them in office. An elected politician is, in this sense, an intermediary, working for his backers against other politicians working for theirs. The political arena is a marketplace as surely as the stock exchange; the absence of a posted money price does not suspend the principles of competition for scarce things, and incentives operate in politics no less than in business.

That reframing drains the meaning out of two words you hear constantly, "anticompetitive" and "procompetitive," used as if one named everything bad and the other everything good. Because scarcity is permanent, so is competition; something always has to be allocated, and the only real question is *which form* of competition does the allocating. Here the thread pays off: when price competition is suppressed, competition does not end, it changes form. People stop bidding with money and start bidding with votes, connections, lobbying, and favors.

There is a quiet cost in that switch. A price set by buyers and sellers freely dealing reveals how much a thing is worth to people, because they gave up real resources to get it; hand the scarce thing out by political criteria instead and that information is gone. It also explains why "free" public services keep generating shortages: the famous lament about "private abundance and public squalor" is partly an artifact of public goods priced so low they perpetually appear scarce while privately sold goods, priced to clear, do not. The contrast is manufactured by the pricing.

## Cartels That Last Are the Ones Government Enforces

Politics-as-a-marketplace explains one of the most reliable patterns in the economy. Producers would love to act together to hold output down and prices up, but a private agreement is desperately fragile: every member has an incentive to cheat by shaving the price, and high prices invite outsiders to undercut the cartel and new entrants and substitutes to appear. We will work out why these cartels crumble when we study market power; for now, just take it that voluntary collusion is hard to hold together.

What makes a restriction stick is government. The cartels that endure are backed by law, because the law can do what the colluders cannot do for themselves: bar new entrants and compel the existing producers to stay in line. When European coal producers once pooled their sales through a single agency, the reason no producer could simply opt out and undersell the group was that the law required them to join. Strip away the legal compulsion and the cheating and entry that wreck private collusion take back over.

Once you see the pattern, ask the question we will use on every policy from here on: who benefits, and who pays? Government-erected barriers to entry transfer wealth from the many to the few. A handful of incumbent producers, concentrated and politically organized, capture what economists carefully call monopoly *rents*, the gains that come purely from restricting supply rather than from producing more or better, while the cost is spread thinly across a vast number of consumers who each pay a little more and never notice. Concentrated benefits, dispersed costs: that asymmetry is why such policies persist even when they make the country poorer, because the few who gain a lot fight hard and the many who each lose a little do not bother.

## Farm Programs and Licensing Are Politics in Action

Farmers have been politically powerful almost everywhere, and farm policy shows what that power buys. Japan has long kept out imported rice to prop up its rice farmers, so Japanese consumers pay dearly for it, and Europe's Common Agricultural Policy is notorious for pushing food prices well above what open competition would deliver. The United States restricts imports of sugar, peanuts, and other products to hold up the prices its domestic producers receive, so Americans face less supply and higher prices. The dairy program is the cleanest illustration of a tool we already studied: the government sets a price floor above the market level and forbids selling below it. A price floor produces a surplus, so the government ends up either capping how many cows farmers may keep or buying up the unsold milk and cheese, which then gets stored, given away, or left to spoil. That is our price-floor mechanism playing out as national policy.

Diagnose these programs the way the course has trained you: strip off the stated objective, food security or helping farmers, and look at the realized outcome and who is served. After decades of subsidies the honest justification has largely worn away, and what keeps the programs alive is closer to the campaign contributions the beneficiaries make to the officials who vote for them, the same concentrated-benefits, dispersed-costs logic.

Licensing is the same machine in a different industry. When the state requires an expensive license to braid hair, it fences out potential competitors and protects those already inside, exactly as an import quota fences out foreign producers. The principle is blunt: restricting competitors to keep output low and prices high is illegal when private firms do it alone and perfectly legal when the government is a member of the arrangement.

Labor unions fit the same frame, and I will keep this brief because the wage analysis comes in the labor topic. A union is, in economic terms, collusion that the law permits. Decades ago the federal government granted unions the legal right to strike and restrain competition from non-union workers, and later the exclusive authority to represent everyone in a workplace, members or not. That is a legally backed restraint on the competition non-union labor would otherwise supply, which is why many states have pushed back with right-to-work laws. For now, treat it as one more government-enabled limit on competition.

## The Environment Is Not Markets Versus Nature

One myth deserves its own diagnosis: the idea that there is a war between "the economy" and "the environment," with markets as the villain that fouls the air and water. Ralph Nader once noted the apparent injustice that a man can be fined for relieving himself in a river while large industries pollute it freely. The contrast feels damning, but it misses the economics. Pollution is not a free-standing evil that producers commit out of malice; it is a by-product of producing things we value, electricity, steel, the goods of modern life, and it imposes a real cost on others. The problem is not that production happens; it is that no one owns the river, so its use as a dumping ground carries no price and nobody bears the cost of fouling it. The economic answer is not zero-tolerance prohibition, which would also wipe out the valuable output, but making the polluter bear the cost, in effect attaching a price to the right to pollute so the activity continues only where it is worth more than the harm it does.

That is the deeper lesson from our property-rights unit: a great many apparent "market failures" turn out to be missing or unassigned property rights, cases of there being no owner and no price for the scarce thing, not cases of markets producing bad results. "Economics versus the environment" is a false opposition. The question is how to price the scarce thing so its use reflects its true cost, not whether to have an economy at all.

## Economic Reasoning Is a Tool, Not an Ideology

People often dismiss economics as just one ideology among others, a set of opinions that favor business or the rich. That gets the discipline exactly backwards. Consider two skilled economists testifying on opposite sides of a dispute. You might expect rival economic theories; in fact they are using the same validated principles, and what they disagree about is the facts of the case, or which outcomes to weigh more heavily. We saw this on day one: a committed socialist and a committed free-marketeer can reason about incentives and costs in the very same way and still want completely different things. They part company over values and facts, not over how the economy works.

That is why the tool is genuinely value-neutral and applies to any institutions, not just to capitalism. The claim that "economic theory only applies to a capitalist society" is false; the analysis of how scarce resources get allocated under competition holds under socialism too, where pricing things below their value generates shortages for the same reasons a price ceiling does. Suppress price competition and you do not abolish competition, you relocate it to politics, in Moscow and Washington alike.

## Economics Clarifies Your Values, It Does Not Replace Them

I want to close where I closed our very first day. People wave economics away with "yes, but there are also non-economic values to consider." The truth is the reverse. There are *only* non-economic values, and economics supplies none of them. It never tells you to make the most money possible; it is only a method for weighing whatever you already care about against everything else, because scarcity means you cannot have it all. So "there are also non-economic values" usually turns out to be a polite refusal to let one's own pet value be weighed against anything, even though the resources that go into saving a scenic lake might instead have vaccinated thousands of children. Pretending the trade-off does not exist will not make it disappear. The honest meaning of "no free lunch" is that the choice gets made either way; refusing to weigh it just hands the choice to default.

Watch how the language hides who pays. The standard rhetorical move is to describe one side of a transaction in human terms and the other as "the market" or some faceless intermediary. A newspaper columnist complains that faceless Wall Street analysts force profit requirements on his paper, as if the analysts were the villains; but the money those analysts steer is not their own, it is the savings and pension funds of millions of ordinary people. Asking a paper to accept a lower return is asking those people to accept a smaller pension so that editors, who often out-earn them, can keep doing journalism a certain way. Put plainly, it is teachers and mechanics subsidizing reporters, and the rhetoric works only by leaving the teachers and mechanics out of the picture. The same trick runs through a thousand appeals: a writer laments that the factory will not magnanimously give one woman the day shift she wants, but magnanimity here just means forcing some other worker onto the night shift to bear her cost, a cost the writer himself pays none of. Demanding generosity with other people's burdens is not generosity. Whenever you hear that the market should be more humane, ask which humans are being kept off the page, because that is usually where the cost has been hidden.

The hardest version, met on the first day, is human life. "If it saves even one life, it is worth any cost" cannot survive scrutiny: the resources a costly rule swallows have other life-saving uses, and wealth itself saves lives, which is why the same earthquake kills a handful of people in a rich country and thousands in a poor one. Whatever a life is worth, it is not infinite, and a policy that saves one life at the cost of several others is not the bargain it pretends to be.

One last habit guards against bad moral reasoning: distinguish what merely *emerges* from an institution from what is *caused* by it. Stores in poor neighborhoods often charge more, but that reflects the higher cost of doing business there, not gouging; the most advanced hospitals show higher death rates because they take the cases no one else can handle, not because they are worse. Distinguish, too, achievements from privileges: a privilege comes at someone else's expense, while an achievement, an invention, a cure, a cheaper way to make something, adds to everyone's benefit even as it makes the inventor rich. Moral judgments require accurate facts and honest causal reasoning, which is just Justice Holmes's old advice to think things, not words. Markets are not amoral; they let individuals act on their own values and then bear the cost. The real question is never markets versus morality. It is whose values get imposed on, or paid for by, other people.

## The Toolkit You Are Leaving With

That is the course. We started with scarcity and the discovery that competition is unavoidable, so the only real choice is its form, and we end by watching that idea do its work: the market myths dissolved under tools you already had, and the political-economy lens, that politics is a marketplace where suppressing price competition only forces it into political channels, let us see who gains and who pays from cartels, farm programs, and licensing. Underneath all of it sits the very first thing we learned about exchange: when two people trade voluntarily, both walk away better off by their own reckoning, which is why a market is positive-sum and not the zero-sum fight it is so often mistaken for. The price system does some things superbly and others poorly, and where it falls short the political alternatives tend to fail in their own way, by hiding the trade-off and serving the organized few at the scattered many's expense.

There is one more habit worth carrying out the door, because it keeps the whole toolkit honest. An economy is a single connected system, not a row of separate problems to be fixed one at a time, so whenever someone proposes a law aimed at one problem the right reflex is to ask what else moves. Cap a price to help renters and you have not just lowered a number; you have shrunk the supply of housing, redirected building elsewhere, raised costs for everything that depends on it, and rewarded whoever already holds a lease. The repercussions do not stop at the problem the policy named; they ripple through the rest of the system, often undoing the very thing the policy was meant to do. A fix that looks clean in isolation rarely stays clean once the connected parts respond, which is why "compared to what, and what else moves?" is the core of thinking about any policy.

The point was never to make you cheer for markets or against government. It was to make you ask the questions a careful person asks: Compared to what? Who gains and who pays? What does this policy actually do, as opposed to what it is called? Bring those questions to whatever you read after this class is over, and you will find they cut against comfortable opinions on every side. The toolkit is yours now.
