# Tax Incidence and the Politics of Controls

A price left free settles where the quantity people want to buy matches the quantity available. Forbid it to move and a gap opens: cap it below that level and a *shortage* appears, prop it up above and a *surplus* appears. We have already traced where the rationing goes once money is barred from doing it, into queues, waiting lists, and favoritism under a ceiling, and into stored or slaughtered surpluses under a floor. This post picks up three threads left hanging by that story.

First, a tax is not a cap, but it raises the same question in a sharper form: when the government takes a slice of a price, who actually pays it, especially when the thing taxed cannot get out of the way? Second, why do prices and policies so often produce ugly results without anyone intending them? Third, if price controls do so much damage, why are they so hard to repeal? That last thread runs back to a pattern worth naming at the outset. Whenever a policy hands a concentrated benefit to a small, organized group while spreading its costs thinly across a scattered public, the organized few will fight to keep it and the diffuse many will barely notice the loss. The same asymmetry drives the scramble for government-granted privileges that economists call *rent-seeking*: because being shielded from competition is worth a great deal to a favored firm and costs each consumer only a little, the favored firm will outspend the public to win and hold the shield. Hold that concentrated-few-against-dispersed-many picture in mind; it is the key to why bad policies persist.

## Where Supply Cannot Respond, the Owner of the Fixed Thing Bears the Tax

We will study taxes in their own right later, but one case belongs here, because it follows cleanly from the logic of prices and supply, and it is the case you will be tested on first: a tax on something whose supply cannot change.

The cleanest example is land, or more precisely the *economic rent* of land. An economic rent is a payment to a resource whose receipt does not affect how much of it exists or how it is used; the land sits there and is farmed or built on the same way whether its rent is high or low. Yet land rent is an economic rent and a genuine cost at the same time, in two senses. From society's point of view it is not needed to bring the land into being, since the land exists regardless. From the user's point of view it is very much a cost, because he must pay it to obtain the use of the land rather than let someone else have it.

<aside class="econ-callout econ-termset">
<p class="econ-callout__label">Key term</p>
<dl class="econ-term-list">
<div><dt>Economic Rent</dt><dd>A payment to a resource whose quantity in existence and use stay the same whether the payment is high, low, or zero.</dd></div>
</dl>
</aside>

Land is the purest case of a fixed supply, and it is worth being clear about why. Many things are fixed in quantity only for a while. A skill can be retrained, a factory rebuilt, a fleet of trucks enlarged, given enough time, so a payment that looks like a pure surplus today may coax more of the resource into being tomorrow. Land is different: no one is manufacturing more of it, and the acres in a given place cannot be increased no matter how high the rent climbs. Being permanently fixed is not even the whole of it. What clinches land as the textbook case is that it is also immobile: it cannot flee. A painting by a famous artist is also in fixed supply, yet its owner can ship it to whatever place taxes it most lightly; land cannot be moved out from under a tax. That immobility, even more than the fixed quantity, is what pins the tax in place.

Now levy a tax on that land rent. Who bears it? The owner of the land, fully, not the tenant who rents it or the developer who builds on it. The reason is that the amount of land cannot shrink in response to the tax. The same acres will be used in the same ways, competed for by the same renters, so the rent renters are willing to pay is unchanged. All the tax does is take a slice of that rent from the owner on its way through. The owner cannot pass it forward, because he cannot reduce the supply to force the price up; the supply was fixed before the tax and is fixed after it.

It helps to walk through the landlord's temptation to pass the tax along, and watch it fail. Suppose he tries to add the new tax to the rent he charges. His tenants can move; the parcels across town are just as available as his, and their owners face the very same tax and likewise cannot conjure extra land to escape it. Because no owner can shrink supply, none of them can lift the market rent, so a landlord who tacks the tax onto his own rent merely watches his tenants decamp to a parcel priced at the going rate. What a tenant will pay is governed by what the land is worth to him in use, and the tax changed none of that: the same location, the same soil, the same access to the city are on offer before the tax and after it. The burden cannot be shifted onto renters, because renters have somewhere else to stand, while the land, and so its owner, has nowhere to hide.

There is a deeper way to see where the tax settles, and it turns on how the price of a durable thing is set. The price of a piece of land is not paid for this year's use alone; it is paid for the whole future stream of rents the land will throw off, gathered into a single figure today. Anything that changes that future stream therefore changes the land's price the moment it is anticipated. When better times are expected for a location, cleaner air, a coming rail stop, a growing city, its price jumps before a single higher rent is actually collected; the good news, as we say, is already priced in. A tax on the land's rent works the same way in reverse. It is a permanent bite out of every future year's rent, so the price a buyer will pay for the land falls by the whole anticipated value of those future tax payments, gathered into today's figure. Economists call this *capitalization*: the tax is capitalized into a lower land price. Whoever owns the land at the instant the tax is announced absorbs the entire loss as the land's value drops; whoever buys afterward pays a price already marked down by the tax and so bears nothing further.

<aside class="econ-callout econ-termset">
<p class="econ-callout__label">Key term</p>
<dl class="econ-term-list">
<div><dt>Capitalization</dt><dd>The fall in a durable asset's price today when a permanent tax on its future earnings is gathered into a single present figure.</dd></div>
</dl>
</aside>

That gives the incidence a shape you could look for in the world. Raise the tax on land, and the price of land falls by roughly the capitalized value of the added tax, while the acres in use, and the uses they are put to, scarcely budge. That is the signature of a tax no one can dodge: the burden shows up as a one-time drop in the asset's price rather than as a smaller quantity or a higher price to users. It is the mirror image of the good-news case, where an anticipated improvement showed up as a jump in the land's price with no change in the dirt itself.

<p class="econ-callout__label">Pause and check</p>
<p>A town doubles its tax on land rent. Using the capitalization logic, predict what happens to the price of land, to the number of acres actually in use, and to who bears the burden: the owner at the moment the tax is announced or someone who buys the land afterward.</p>
</aside>

Two honest qualifications keep this from being a fairy tale, and both come from taking the fixed-supply assumption seriously. First, land is not quite perfectly fixed or perfectly costless to hold: swamps can be drained and bays filled to make new land, and some sites must be maintained against erosion, so a tax heavy enough to swallow those returns would, at the margin, discourage even those activities. Second, the clean result holds exactly only for the part of the payment that is a true rent, the part that would be there whether or not anyone was paid. Even granting those caveats, a tax on land distorts behavior far less than a tax on almost anything else, precisely because most of what land earns is a rent that no tax can chase away. That property was tempting enough that reformers once dreamed of funding the whole of government from a single tax on land, on the theory that it alone could be collected without discouraging the activity taxed. Whether that is wise is a question of values; that such a tax is unusually hard to escape is a matter of the analysis.

This case is special precisely because the supply cannot respond. The general question of who really bears a tax when both sides can adjust, when sellers can offer less and buyers can buy less, is a two-sided tug of war we cannot settle yet, because it needs the market supply curve we have not built. We will take it up later, once we have that curve in hand. For now, learn the clean result: a tax on a truly fixed supply lands on the owner of the fixed thing.

We have leaned on an idea here that we will build properly much later: that the price of a durable thing today already reflects what is expected of it tomorrow. Hold that thought. It returns, with the machinery to make it precise, when we study how present and future values are linked.

## Bad Outcomes Are Usually the System Working, Not Someone Being Stupid or Evil

It is tempting to explain high prices and failed policies by pointing at villains: greedy sellers, corrupt officials, stupid bureaucrats. Economics pushes you toward a less satisfying but more accurate habit, looking for *systemic* causes rather than *intentional* ones, for the incentives that make ordinary people produce an outcome whether they intend it or not.

Take the charge of greed. When prices rise after a shock, the cause is the collision of supply and demand, not a sudden outbreak of avarice that conveniently coincides with the shock. Prices in low-income neighborhoods are a sharper test. Groceries, loans, and check-cashing often cost more there, which looks like exploitation. But the per-dollar cost of doing business is genuinely higher: an armored car costs the same to serve a small check-casher as a big bank, theft and default run higher, and volume is lower, so each transaction carries more overhead. The higher price reflects higher cost, not greater greed. When lawmakers respond by capping prices or interest rates, legitimate lenders and stores withdraw, leaving residents to loan sharks and long bus rides, worse than what the cap was meant to fix.

The same lens applies to government failure. When a program backfires, the cause is rarely that officials are idiots; far more often they are rational people responding sensibly to the incentives they face, which do not line up with the public good. A planner rewarded for meeting a quota measured in tons will make heavy, useless goods. Subsidies and taxes feed the same machine by distorting the prices that are supposed to tell the truth about scarcity. When water is sold to favored users far below its value, they treat it as nearly free and waste it, growing thirsty crops in deserts while others pay many times as much for the same water. The waste is not a moral failing of the farmers; it is the rational response to a price rigged to lie about how scarce the water really is.

## Price Controls Persist Because Their Costs Are Hidden and Their Beneficiaries Are Organized

If price controls do such damage, why are they so durable? The answer is political economy, and it ties this topic together.

First, controls are popular at the start. The morning a price is capped, buyers see a low price and cheer; the shortages, lines, and deterioration come later, and few connect them back to the cap. When one government ordered prices slashed in half, the immediate effect was an ecstatic shopping spree, followed within weeks by bare shelves and an economy at a standstill. The wider the gap a control forces between the legal price and the market price, the more severe the eventual damage, but that damage is delayed and diffuse while the benefit is instant and visible.

Second, the gains are concentrated and the losses dispersed. A control hands a clear, sizable benefit to an organized group, sitting tenants under rent control, established farmers under a price support, who know exactly what they are getting and will fight to keep it. The costs fall on a scattered mass, would-be tenants who never find an apartment, taxpayers who fund the surplus, consumers who pay a bit more, each losing too little to mount a campaign. The organized beneficiaries outlast the diffuse victims, the very concentrated-winners-against-dispersed-losers asymmetry behind the rent-seeking we named at the outset.

<aside class="econ-callout econ-check">
<p class="econ-callout__label">Pause and check</p>
<p>A city council proposes repealing rent control. Which group is most likely to pack the meeting to defend it, and why do the people who would gain from repeal barely show up?</p>
</aside>

Third, controls hand politicians something valuable. The power to set and enforce a price is the power to decide who gets the scarce good, a form of authority worth a great deal, and it lets officials claim credit for low prices while blaming the resulting shortages on hoarders, speculators, or greedy sellers. The damage is real but deniable; the credit is immediate and personal.

Through all of it, keep the economist's discipline in view: this is positive analysis, not a verdict. Scarcity always forces some form of competition. If competition by money price is suppressed, competition by force, by waiting, by connections, by political pull takes its place; the competition never stops, only its form changes. Economics can tell you what each form of competition will do, who will gain and who will bear the cost. It cannot tell you which scarce goods *ought* to be rationed by markets and which by some other rule. That choice rests on values you bring to it. What economics offers is the cause and effect, so that whatever you decide, you decide it with your eyes open.
