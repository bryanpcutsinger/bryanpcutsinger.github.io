# The Economic Way of Thinking

Most people assume economics is about money: budgets, the stock market, how to get rich. That is not what this course is about. What I want to hand you is a way of thinking, a small set of tools you will keep using long after the final exam. You will reach for them when you read the news, when you make your own decisions, and when you weigh the claims people make about how the world ought to work. By the end of this post you should be able to say what economics actually studies, why every society runs into the same unavoidable problem, and why thinking like an economist has surprisingly little to do with money.

Underneath it all sits one stubborn fact: there is never enough. Not enough time, money, doctors, parking spaces, or clean air to give everyone everything they want. That shortfall is not a problem we will eventually solve. It is the permanent condition of human life, and it forces a question on every society: who gets what, and who decides?

Economics is how we reason about that question.

## Economics Is About Scarce Resources, Not Money

The classic definition, which we owe to the economist [Lionel Robbins](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Robbins.html), is short:

<aside class="econ-callout econ-def">
<p class="econ-callout__label">Definition</p>
<p>Economics is the study of how people use scarce resources that have alternative uses.</p>
</aside>

Both halves carry weight. *Scarce* means there is not enough to go around. *Alternative uses* means each resource could serve more than one purpose. An hour of your time could go to studying, sleeping, or a paying shift; a barrel of oil could become fuel, plastic, or fertilizer. Because resources are both limited and usable in many ways, every time we commit them to one purpose we give up the others.

<aside class="econ-callout econ-termset">
<p class="econ-callout__label">Key terms</p>
<dl class="econ-term-list">
<div><dt>Scarcity</dt><dd>our wants exceed what is available.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Alternative Uses</dt><dd>the same resource could be used in more than one way.</dd></div>
</dl>
</aside>

Notice that money appears nowhere in that definition. For a society as a whole, money is just a tool for moving real things around. If printing it made a nation rich, no country would ever be poor. The real economy is the goods, services, time, and effort that money stands for, not the paper itself. A decision can even be economic with no money at all. A medical team that reaches a disaster with more wounded than it can treat must decide who gets care first: scarcity, alternative uses, and a hard trade-off, with not a cent changing hands. Economics is not a guide to personal finance. It is the study of how scarce resources get put to use.

## Economics Traces Cause and Effect, and Judges Policies by Results

If economics is not a manual for making money, what is it? It is a science: a careful effort to work out what actually happens when people and governments do particular things. The emphasis falls on cause and effect, not on opinion or good intentions. This view of economics has two implications, and both are worth knowing from the start.

First, this way of thinking does not belong to a political party. Economists at opposite ends of the spectrum analyze the mechanics of an economy in much the same way. A committed socialist like [Oskar Lange](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Lange.html) and a free-market champion like [Milton Friedman](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Friedman.html) reached for the same analytical tools, however bitterly they disagreed about what the economy ought to do. They parted company over what they wanted, not over how incentives and costs behave.

Second, judge a policy by its consequences rather than its intentions. Good intentions are cheap, and anyone can have them. What matters is what a policy actually does, including its effects years later and on people nobody had in mind. A law meant to help can end up hurting, and a measure that sounds cold can end up saving lives. The only way to tell which is which is to trace the cause and effect all the way through. The stakes are not abstract. When China shifted toward more market-friendly policies after 1978, [hundreds of millions of people climbed out of severe poverty](https://humanprogress.org/explaining-chinas-economic-miracle/) within a single generation, a change that followed directly from a change in the rules.

## Scarcity Is Permanent, So Trade-offs Are Unavoidable

Scarcity is not unique to any particular society; it is an ever-present condition. It is not the result of producing the "wrong" things, either. People often feel we would have plenty if we just stopped making frivolous goods. But scarcity comes from a plain fact: human wants outrun any quantity of means, and rearranging what we produce does not make the shortfall disappear.

It helps to picture the alternative. Imagine the Garden of Eden, where everything anyone could want is simply there for the taking. Such a place would have production and consumption, but no economy and no economics, because no one would ever have to choose.

And scarcity is not a problem only for the poor. A [news feature once profiled middle-class American families](https://fortune.com/2023/05/18/is-100000-salary-six-figure-enough-for-henry-millennials/), people who would have counted as richly prosperous by the standards of almost any earlier century, as barely getting by, their wants still outrunning even comfortable incomes. What pressed on them was not a budget someone could loosen. It was reality.

We need to distinguish between two types of goods. An *economic good* is scarce enough that getting more of it means giving something up. The word "good" here carries no seal of approval. Something counts as a good if anyone wants more of it than they can freely have, whether or not you would ever buy it yourself; tobacco is an economic good even to a nonsmoker. A *free good*, by contrast, is so abundant that nobody wants more even at a price of zero, and those turn out to be rare. Ordinary air is the usual example, until you are a scuba diver or an astronaut, or you live in a city choking on smog.

One point deserves special attention, because it comes up later and causes a lot of confusion: a price tag of $0 does not make something free. The money price of "free" parking, "free" beaches, and "free" tuition is zero, but the resources they use are every bit as scarce and costly to provide as anything you pay for. Worse, handing a scarce thing out at no charge usually makes its scarcity bite harder, because nothing restrains how much people take. Somebody still pays, in waiting, in lines, in showing up before dawn. Always ask who pays, and in what form.

It also is not enough to have resources. What matters is how well you turn them into things people want, and that capacity is what we mean by productivity. It accounts for much of the gap between a rich country and a poor one. During the Cold War, Soviet industry often used more electricity than American industry while producing less. The reverse is just as telling: Japan and Switzerland have very little in the way of natural resources, yet enjoy high standards of living, while some countries blessed with resources stay poor. Economists call that pattern the [resource curse](https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/curse-or-blessing-how-institutions-determine-success-resource-rich-economies), and what really does the work behind it is whether a country's institutions are good or bad. A nation's wealth has less to do with what it starts with than with how well it puts it to use.

## Every Society Needs a Rule for Who Gets What

Because scarce things cannot satisfy everyone who wants them, people compete for them. Competition is not a quirk of capitalism that we could erase with better attitudes. It is a consequence of scarcity, and it would exist in a world with no money, prices, or markets at all. The only thing open to choice is the form the competition takes.

Take a concrete case. A campus has 5,000 students and 2,000 parking spaces. Some get a space and some do not, which is not a policy choice but a matter of arithmetic, so the campus needs a rule for deciding who.

Societies have several options available: brute force, political power (whoever the state favors), authority (a committee picks who is "most deserving"), first-come-first-served, or exchange (whoever is willing to pay). Political power is on the list because the state holds a near-monopoly on legitimate force; it can compel people in ways no private party can, which is exactly why control of it is worth fighting over. That same monopoly on force is what lets a government make and enforce the rules and settle disputes between people, though it is never the only thing that does the job: custom, private arbitration, and the fear of a ruined reputation settle far more quarrels than any court.

Every one of these rules carries a cost, even the ones that look free. First-come-first-served makes you pay in time, dragging yourself to campus before sunrise. A "most deserving" committee makes people burn effort proving they qualify. No method escapes cost; they just change who pays and how.

Which forms of competition are even allowed shifts from one arena to another. In a private business you compete by offering a better product at a lower price, plain selling for money. In a race for political office you can do something a business legally cannot: you can promise to use the state's power over other people's wealth, pledging to raise taxes on some and cut them for others, or to steer a program toward those who vote for you. The permitted tactic changes with the arena, and noticing which tactic is in play tells you a lot about how a given prize is really won.

The rule in force often depends, too, on who owns the thing being handed out. A private downtown garage rations parking by charging money, because the owner gets to keep what is paid. A public university rations admission by grades, test scores, residence, alumni ties, and the like. Part of the reason is that no one owns the university in a way that lets them personally benefit from admitting a student. When no one can benefit monetarily from the choice, price cannot do the rationing, so non-price criteria move in to do it instead. So whenever you see a scarce thing handed out, ask which rule is running, who can compete and how, and what it is costing people.

The course mostly studies one of these rules: exchange. Trading is competitive and cooperative at once. Rivals bid against each other for your business, yet a deal closes only when it leaves both sides better off. You compete for a deal by serving the person on the other side.

## Every Choice Has a Cost

Every choice closes off the things you did not pick, and the most valuable of those is the real cost of what you choose. Economists call it the *opportunity cost*. Money may not enter into it at all.

Consider a shopkeeper who tells a girl named Annie she can have any candy in the store, free. Annie objects that it is not really free. To take the candy she wants most, she has to pass up the one she would have reached for second, and that second choice is what the first one costs her. She was handed the chance to choose at no charge. The choosing itself still cost her something.

This idea has a few features that regularly trip people up.

- **Cost is personal.** It is measured against whatever you yourself would otherwise have done, so your cost and your roommate's for the same choice can differ.
- **Cost looks forward.** Money already spent is a *sunk cost* and should carry no weight now. Twenty hours poured into a term paper that is not working is no reason to keep going; the only question is what your best move is from here.
- **Cost is more than money.** It includes the value of the time a choice eats up. Spend an hour shopping to save three dollars when that hour was worth ten to you, and you have come out behind.

Two of these costs have names worth knowing. An *explicit cost* is one you pay out in money: the rent, the wages, the price on the tag. An *implicit cost* is a value you give up without any payment changing hands, and it is the one people overlook. Suppose Joe runs his repair shop out of a building he already owns, so he writes no rent check. He still bears a real cost, because he could have leased that building to someone else; the rent he gives up by using it himself is an implicit cost. Add the explicit and the implicit together and you have the *full cost* of a choice, which is just the opportunity cost counted in full. The hour you spend studying carries a full cost too: not only any money it takes, but the value of whatever else you would have done with the time.

The unpleasant byproducts of a choice are not its cost. People say the cost of playing tennis is a sore arm, or that the cost of studying is eyestrain. The sore arm and the eyestrain are consequences of the activity, not its cost. The cost of an hour spent studying is the best thing you gave up to spend it that way, perhaps an hour of a game you would have enjoyed. Bad side effects can weigh on whether you do something, but they are not the cost.

One last idea belongs here, and it is where our work on demand will begin: the more of something you already have, the less you will give up to get one more unit of it. To someone dying of thirst, the first glass of water is worth almost anything. By the fifth glass, it is worth very little.

## Efficiency Means No Waste

People throw the word "efficient" around as if it meant "produce as much as possible." It does not. Picture every combination of two goods an economy's resources could produce, say food and clean air, as a single boundary line. Economists call that boundary the *production possibilities frontier*. Any point on the frontier is efficient: the only way to get more of one good is to give up some of the other. Any point inside is wasteful, because from there you could have more of both at once. So efficiency means no waste, being on the frontier. It does not mean having the most of any single thing.

"Maximize one good" is an incoherent goal. Grow nothing but food, working every acre and running every factory flat out, and the air turns to smog. Shut all of that down for the sake of clean air, and you would breathe beautifully with nothing to eat. We do not want the most of any one thing; we want some workable combination, and efficiency is what forces us to face the trade-off.

How far out the frontier sits is not fixed forever. Better tools, new technology, and sounder institutions can push the whole frontier outward, so that a society can have more food and more clean air at once. That outward shift is what we mean by economic growth, and it is the only way to escape a trade-off without giving something up. Scarcity does not vanish, though; the new frontier still bounds what is possible, and every point on it still forces a choice.

That outward push depends on the rules people live under. Secure property, enforceable contracts, and sensible policy move the frontier out, while predatory or chaotic institutions pull it in. That is much of why two countries with similar resources produce wildly different amounts, and it is where economics shades into politics. Being on the frontier still does not tell you which point on it is best. You might trade some food for cleaner air while I would trade the other way, and efficiency alone cannot crown a winner. Efficiency is a no-waste test, not a verdict about what is good, which people forget the instant a policy gets branded "inefficient."

## Markets Rest on Property Rights and Voluntary Exchange

One caricature of economics is worth correcting head-on: the idea that it assumes everyone is selfish. My coauthor Alexander Salter and I have argued at length against exactly this picture ([Cutsinger and Salter, "Putting Economics Back in Humanomics," *Public Choice*, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11127-025-01366-2)). The assumption is not that people are selfish, only that they pursue their own interests, and your interests can easily include the well-being of others. Self-interest is not selfishness. It just means having goals and acting on them.

If people act on their own goals, how do self-interested strangers end up cooperating rather than colliding? Mostly through voluntary exchange. A trade happens only when both sides expect to come out ahead, or one of them would walk away. That has a striking implication: in an ordinary trade both parties gain, and nobody wins at the other's expense. (Economists call this positive-sum.) And because each side has to offer the other something worth having, self-interest gets harnessed to serve people you will never meet. No one even knows how to make a pencil from scratch, from the wood to the graphite to the little brass band, yet by pursuing their own ends, scattered strangers manage to put one in your hand for a few cents. As Adam Smith put it in 1776:

<blockquote class="econ-quote">
<p>It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.</p>
<footer>Adam Smith, <cite>The Wealth of Nations</cite> (1776)</footer>
</blockquote>

None of this works without *property rights*. Owning something means holding three powers at once, and stripping away any one of them guts the ownership:

- **The right to use it.** Without it, someone else decides what your thing is for, so it is not really yours.
- **The right to sell or transfer it.** Without it, the thing cannot move to whoever values it most, and much of the gain from trade is lost.
- **The right to keep what it earns and absorb what it loses.** Without it, no one has any reason to invest in the thing, improve it, or take care of it.

A "right," in this sense, is not your own personal muscle. It is a claim your community recognizes and enforces. Your car is yours because the people around you will back you up if someone tries to take it.

<p class="econ-callout__label">Key term</p>
<dl class="econ-term-list">
<div><dt>Property Rights</dt><dd>socially enforced claims over a scarce resource: the power to use it, transfer it, and keep the gains or bear the losses from what you own.</dd></div>
</dl>

Two things are worth pointing out. The first is about socio-economic systems. The gap between a "capitalist" and a "socialist" society is not whether property rights exist at all; even in the Soviet Union, people owned their clothes and furniture. The difference is how far private rights extend, especially over the productive resources used to make things for others: the factories, farmland, and machines. In principle, a fully socialist economy is one where no private individual holds those three powers over productive property; the state does. That is what "abolishing private property" is really about. It does not mean you cannot own a toothbrush; it means no one privately owns the steel mill. Real economies sit somewhere between the two poles, and the interesting question is always how far private rights over productive property reach. What changes across that border is the rules, not human nature.

The second point is about what a right protects. Your rights protect the physical thing you own, not its market value. If I open a better taco stand across the street and your sales fall, I have taken nothing that was yours; that is just competition. "Free speech" works the same way: it lets you address willing listeners using your own resources, but never lets you commandeer someone else's megaphone.

A related confusion is worth dissolving outright, because it shows up in a hundred political arguments: the slogan that "human rights must take priority over property rights." The opposition is empty. Property has no rights; only people do. Property rights are themselves human rights, namely a person's right to use a scarce resource. Suppose a city orders a landlord to rent his building only to tenants it selects, in the name of a human right to housing. That does not pit "human rights" against cold "property"; it pits the would-be tenant's claim against the owner's, and both of them are people. Saying "human rights win" just hides which person you have decided should lose.

## Good Theories Are Judged by Usefulness, Not Truth

Economists reason with *theories*, and a theory is just a machine for getting from "if this happens" to "then this follows." The question to ask yourself is not whether a theory is true, since none is perfect or complete, but whether it is useful. That is, does it predict well enough, and cheaply enough, for the job at hand? A theory is like a map. Every map leaves things out, the bumps and the billboards, and you would choose a different map for driving than for hiking. A theory earns its keep only if its predictions stick their neck out, only if some evidence could prove them wrong. "Price controls might cause shortages" tells you almost nothing, because "might" rules nothing out. "Price controls raise the odds of shortages" is a prediction you can actually check.

Students often raise two objections. The first is that people are not really that rational. True, but the theory does not need them to be. We are after regularities in how people respond when their options change, and those can hold even when no one is calculating, the way water runs downhill though no single molecule decides to. The economist Gary Becker showed this carefully: the broad market regularities we care about, like the law of demand, survive even when individuals behave erratically ([Becker, "Irrational Behavior and Economic Theory," 1962](https://doi.org/10.1086/258584)). The second objection confuses a theory that hangs together with one that works. A theory can be flawlessly logical and predict badly, or rest on cartoonish assumptions and predict accurately. Whether its parts are internally consistent and whether its predictions survive contact with the world are two separate tests, and the second is the one that counts.

Two warnings about evidence are worth keeping. First, be skeptical of sensational first reports, which often get corrected once the excitement dies down. The lesson is to judge a question by the full body of evidence, the weight of many studies, not one striking paper or headline. Second, watch for *regression to the mean*. Many outcomes are part skill and part luck, so an extreme result, the very best or very worst, is usually followed by a more ordinary one as the luck evens out. A student who bombs one exam tends to score closer to normal on the next, with or without a tutor. This is how useless "cures" earn their reputations: treat the worst cases, watch their scores drift back toward average on their own, and credit the treatment for what was really just luck running out.

## Economics Tells You What Is, Not What Should Be

This is the line between *positive* and *normative* claims. A positive claim is about what is, or what will happen if we do something; a normative claim is about what should be. Economics is a positive, if-this-then-that tool: it can tell you the consequences of a tax or a rent-control law, but not whether those consequences are good. The "should" is yours to supply, once you have seen the "is." Be wary of words that smuggle a "should" inside what sounds like fact, like "fair," "equal," and "socially preferred." "Fair" does not have to mean "equal," and "what society prefers" quietly assumes a group can prefer things, when only individuals do the preferring. Pin down what such a word means before you argue about it. Economics can sharpen the question; it cannot settle it for you.

This is also where you should be most on guard against economists themselves, myself included, and I mean their opinions, not their analysis. What economics says and what an economist recommends are not the same thing. An economist can deduce the likely consequences of a policy more reliably than most people, and that is the real expertise. But the moment one tells you to "cut tariffs" or "buy this stock," that advice has crossed from analysis into preference, and a preference dressed in a credential is no more authoritative than your own. Learning economics buys you a sharper "is," not a better "should." So take the consequences an economist traces out seriously, but treat the recommendation tacked onto the end as just one more opinion to weigh against your own.

## Only Individuals Choose

I just said that only individuals prefer. The same is true of choosing, and it is worth pausing on, because it underlies almost everything we will do with policy. "The government," "the firm," and "the market" have no minds or goals of their own. The people inside them do. A government is really just all the officials, voters, and interest groups who make it up, each responding to their own rewards and pressures.

That is why we keep pushing explanations down to individuals. If we want to understand why "the government" or "a business" does something, we do not ask what "it" wants; we ask what the human beings inside it are rewarded and punished for doing. People respond to the incentives they face, whether they are acting in a market, in a political office, or in any other setting, so following the incentives down to individuals makes a lot of otherwise baffling behavior click into place. This is the hinge between economics and politics, and we will lean on it all term.

## Economics Weighs Your Values; It Doesn't Supply Them

I want to close with what economics has to say about your values, which is less, and more useful, than people expect. People wave the subject away with "yes, but there are also non-economic values to consider." The truth runs the other way. Nearly every value that matters to us is non-economic, and economics supplies none of them. It will not tell you what to care about. It is a method for weighing whatever you already care about against everything else you do. It certainly never orders you to make as much money as possible. An economics professor could almost always earn more in private industry, and a talented doctor could chase the most lucrative specialty instead of the one she loves; nothing in economics tells either of them to go for the bigger paycheck. Many people instead take fortunes earned in the market and pour them into doing good: Andrew Carnegie built free public libraries, John D. Rockefeller founded the University of Chicago, and today the largest technology fortunes bankroll global campaigns against disease.

You cannot avoid weighing the trade-off, because scarcity is always there. There are no costless fixes, only trade-offs, which is the meaning of "no free lunch." Refusing to weigh a trade-off does not make it vanish; the choice still gets made by default, on whatever terms circumstances impose. Insisting that some cherished goal must never be weighed against anything is itself a choice with a cost. Money spent to preserve a scenic lake or a historic building is money that might instead have vaccinated thousands of children somewhere else. We can wish to do it all at once, but the wish does not lift the constraint.

None of this tells us which life-saving policy to choose; it only shows why the trade-off cannot be avoided. The hardest version involves human lives. "If it saves even one life, it is worth any cost" can sound unanswerable. But the resources a costly policy swallows have other life-saving uses. If a regulation spends five times as much to save one life as some other measure would, it has effectively cost five other lives, people we never see but lives all the same. And nobody really acts as if a life were of infinite value. No one would spend half a nation's yearly output to keep one person alive an extra thirty seconds, yet that is what treating a human life as infinitely valuable would demand. People give up safety all the time: they take dangerous jobs when the pay is high enough, and gamble their lives skydiving for fun. Wealth itself saves lives, which is why the same earthquake kills a handful of people in a rich country and thousands in a poor one. So a policy that holds an economy back can cost lives too, even when we never see the bill.

That is why I think of economics as the real art of the possible. Politics often invites us to vote for several things that cannot all happen at once. Economics is the discipline that keeps pointing at the trade-off and asking "compared to what?" That is not cynicism. It is what taking your own values seriously requires.

<h3>Key takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Scarcity is permanent.</strong> Wants outrun the resources available to satisfy them, so every society has to choose who gets what.</li>
<li><strong>Trace cause and effect.</strong> Economics judges a policy by its consequences, not its intentions, and follows those consequences all the way through.</li>
<li><strong>Every choice has an opportunity cost:</strong> the most valuable alternative you give up, counted in time and forgone value, not just money.</li>
<li><strong>A $0 money price is not free.</strong> Someone still pays, in waiting, effort, lines, or taxes.</li>
<li><strong>Efficiency means no waste,</strong> being on the frontier, not producing the most of any one thing; growth shifts the whole frontier outward.</li>
<li><strong>Markets rest on property rights and voluntary exchange,</strong> which is positive-sum: both sides expect to gain, or the trade does not happen.</li>
<li><strong>Positive is not normative.</strong> Analysis tells you what is or what will follow; the "should" is a separate value judgment you supply.</li>
<li><strong>Only individuals choose,</strong> so explain an institution by the incentives facing the people inside it.</li>
<li><strong>Economics weighs your values;</strong> it helps you compare the trade-offs among the things you care about, but it does not supply the values themselves.</li>
</ul>
